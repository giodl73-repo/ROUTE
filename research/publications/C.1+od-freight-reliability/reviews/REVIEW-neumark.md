## Overall

The paper claims a specific, quantified economic benefit from PTI improvement — a reduction in the shipper planning buffer from 80 hours to 48 hours — but does not connect that claim to a shipper-level economic gain in dollars. The gap between "PTI improves" and "shippers gain $X" is the central economic question, and the paper does not address it with adequate rigor. That said, the corridor analysis is competent and the I-69 operational benefit claim is well-supported. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The O-D pairing and corridor decomposition are well-motivated. The identification of the Dallas interchange as the binding node for HOU-CHI routing — responsible for a 240-mile routing detour relative to a direct I-69 path — is a strong, concrete finding that does not depend on the PTI model. The I-69 completion case is effectively the paper's most reliable result: shorter distance, fewer bottleneck nodes, and lower transit time are all direct and credible.

The SLA framework is an appropriate way to translate PTI into a shipper decision variable. Shippers plan around commitment windows, not average travel times, so the 80-hour→48-hour compression is the right unit of analysis for a freight reliability paper. The framing is intuitive and would resonate with both academic and policy audiences.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper does not complete the economic argument for the I2.0 investment. Moving from PTI=1.86 to PTI=1.15 on the Bay Area segment compresses the NY-LA SLA window from 80 hours to 48 hours. This is a 40% reduction in the shipper's planning buffer. But what is that worth, in dollars, to the shipper — and to whom does that value accrue?

The $5.7B annual reliability cost figure represents the cost of current unreliability. If I2.0 managed lanes reduce PTI from 1.86 to 1.15, the paper implies that a substantial portion of that $5.7B becomes recoverable. But the paper does not state: (a) what fraction of the $5.7B is recoverable under the PTI target scenario, (b) whether the benefit accrues to shippers (lower inventory cost), carriers (higher truck utilization), or consumers (lower delivered price), and (c) whether the managed lane toll cost offsets some portion of the reliability gain for freight carriers.

A managed lane with PTI=1.15 will carry a toll. If the toll for a NY-LA cross-country managed lane trip is, say, $300–$600 (at $0.10–$0.20/mile on the managed segment), some carriers will choose not to use it, leaving their PTI unchanged. The take-up rate — and the distribution of reliability benefit across freight markets — is a necessary part of the economic case.

## The Question I'd Push On

For a carrier operating the NY-LA corridor 5 days per week, what is the annual dollar benefit of moving from PTI=1.86 to PTI=1.15, net of managed lane tolls, and how does that compare to the per-carrier cost of the managed lane infrastructure? The paper needs at least one worked example at the carrier level — not just the corridor-wide aggregate — to demonstrate that the I2.0 investment produces a net benefit to the individual shipper, not just a transfer of reliability cost from aggregate statistics to a toll revenue stream.
