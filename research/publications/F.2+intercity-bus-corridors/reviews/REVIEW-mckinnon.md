## Overall

The managed lane access question is the paper's most fundamental unresolved issue from a freight logistics perspective. The paper correctly identifies that bus operator access to freight-dedicated managed lanes is not guaranteed, but does not propose a mechanism for resolving this. From the freight carrier's perspective, managed lane throughput is a capacity resource they have paid for through dedicated freight tolls or user fees embedded in the I2.0 investment model. Allowing intercity bus operators to share managed lane capacity without a clear allocation mechanism — one that protects freight throughput during peak periods — will generate carrier opposition that could block bus access entirely. The paper needs to propose an access allocation model, not just acknowledge the gap.

## What Works

The PTI 1.15 threshold is as relevant to freight as to passenger operations, and the paper correctly uses it as the bridge between freight infrastructure investment and bus operator viability. The managed lanes produce PTI 1.15; PTI 1.15 enables timetabled scheduling; timetabled scheduling enables reliable passenger service. This causal chain is sound and the paper presents it cleanly.

The 62 mph average speed figure (contested by Walker on stop-penalty grounds) is at least consistent with what managed lane cruising speeds would permit without intermediate stops — the paper's speed claim is not unreasonable as an upper bound for limited-stop express service. The operational problem Walker identifies is real, but it is fixable by distinguishing limited-stop express service (62 mph achievable) from all-stops local service (lower average speed).

The break-even fare analysis ($0.12-0.14/mile at 45% load factor, $2.80/bus-mile operating cost) is the paper's most transparent quantitative section and the figures are consistent with industry cost data. The $3.1B annual revenue estimate is appropriately framed as contingent on demand assumptions.

## What Doesn't Work

The managed lane allocation mechanism is absent. From a freight system design perspective, this is not a minor detail — it is a fundamental question about whether bus operations and freight operations are compatible uses of the same infrastructure. The allocation design must address: (a) time-of-day access windows (bus priority in off-peak freight periods vs. freight priority during peak shipping windows); (b) speed compatibility (buses at 62 mph average in mixed freight traffic at 65 mph are compatible; buses making intermediate stops at 40 mph in freight managed lanes are not); (c) the regulatory or contractual framework that grants bus operators access rights. Without this, the managed lane access claim is an architectural aspiration rather than a design.

The decarbonization benefit of T1 bus is not computed. Intercity bus is already the most carbon-efficient per-passenger-mile mode in the US surface transportation system (approximately 30-35g CO2/passenger-mile vs. 85-90g for car travel at average occupancy). A T1 bus corridor attracting 24M passengers from auto-mode and air-mode travel would produce a quantifiable emissions reduction that the paper should calculate. This is relevant to the program's policy case in a 2026-2035 environment where transportation decarbonization is a major federal policy priority.

The EV bus transition is not addressed. The 2035-2055 Phase 3 period will see significant electric coach deployment — zero-emission coaches are already commercially available (BYD, Proterra, NFI). The paper should note whether the T1 bus network design is compatible with electric coach operations (charging infrastructure, range requirements on longer corridors, charging dwell-time integration with scheduling).

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper proposes bus service on managed freight lanes. The managed lane design standard (from E.1 and E.2) is optimized for Class 8 freight truck dimensions and operating speeds. What is the geometric compatibility between bus operations (typically 45 ft coaches, lower profile, different wheel-path) and freight operations (53 ft trailers, higher CG, different wheel-path) in managed lane cross-sections? If the managed lane design requires modification to accommodate mixed bus-freight operations, that cost belongs in the I2.0 capital program, not in the bus operator's cost structure.
