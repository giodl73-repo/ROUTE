## Overall
The paper is well-constructed and the economic cases are compelling. The policy section (Section 7) is the weakest part of a paper that otherwise earns its place in JEP — it proposes three policy actions but is vague about the statutory authority, the funding mechanism, and the implementation timeline for each. JEP policy papers need precision on what authority exists, what new authority is required, and who does what when.

## What Works

**The "Layer 0" sequencing insight is the paper's most important policy contribution.** The observation that relay stations ($40M for NY-LA) can be built on current infrastructure and deliver 88% SLA before managed lanes exist is a genuine policy finding. Infrastructure investment discussions rarely distinguish between enabling investments and primary investments; this paper does so correctly and the implications for phasing federal funding are real.

**The relay hub / airline crew base analogy is exactly right for a policy audience.** JEP's readers include economists who don't know trucking but do know how hub-and-spoke airline operations work. The crew base analogy translates the relay concept into a familiar institutional form. This will be the most-cited paragraph in the paper for policy practitioners.

**The simulation results are used correctly.** The paper draws on C.1's Monte Carlo results rather than re-deriving them, and the four-scenario comparison (relay/GP vs relay/I2.0 managed lanes) is presented clearly. The p50/p75/p95 table in Section 7 is essential context for the 88% vs 98.8% SLA comparison.

**The freight network operator concept is the right institutional innovation.** The suggestion to create a neutral relay station operator — analogous to an airport authority — addresses the coordination problem correctly. No single carrier can fill eight relay stations continuously; shared infrastructure requires a shared operator. The trackage rights analogy from rail is apt.

## What Doesn't Work

**The NFRZ designation authority is asserted but not demonstrated.** Section 7.1 claims FHWA can designate National Freight Relay Zones under the National Highway Freight Program (FAST Act / IIJA). This may be correct, but it requires legal analysis to confirm. The NHFP designates the Primary Freight Network, Critical Rural Freight Corridors, and Critical Urban Freight Corridors — it is not obvious that "relay zone" designation falls within existing NHFP authority without a statutory amendment. The paper should either (a) cite specific NHFP statutory language that supports this designation, or (b) acknowledge that new legislation may be required and scope what that legislation would look like.

**The Freight Network Operator framework needs a developer.** Section 7.3 recommends that FHWA, STB, and FMCSA develop the FNO framework jointly. Three agencies with overlapping jurisdiction and different authorizing statutes is not a project plan — it is an aspiration. Which agency leads? Under what rulemaking authority? What is the timeline? JEP papers don't need regulatory implementation plans, but they do need enough specificity that the proposal is distinguishable from "someone should do something."

**The $40M capex figure is for construction only.** The relay station budget in Section 7 covers construction costs ($5M per station). It does not address: land acquisition (relay stations need 5–10 acres at interstate interchanges, where land values are high); access road construction (not captured in the $5M); permitting and environmental review (NEPA for a truck facility at an interstate interchange can take 2–4 years and $2–5M per site). The $40M figure understates true development cost meaningfully and will be challenged by anyone who has permitted a truck facility.

## The Question I'd Push On
Does existing IIJA authority allow FHWA to designate National Freight Relay Zones and fund station infrastructure through the National Highway Performance Program — or does this require new legislation? The answer determines whether this is a near-term executive action or a multi-year legislative project, which changes the entire policy timeline.
