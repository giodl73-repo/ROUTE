## Overall

The paper makes the economic case for a managed freight lane program and identifies a plausible funding mechanism. The policy implications are well-targeted — the PROTECT/NHPP gap and the case for a new federal freight lane program category are real gaps in the current IIJA framework. My concerns are about the feasibility of the transponder toll funding mechanism and the P3 delivery model, which the paper treats as straightforward but which face significant institutional barriers.

Score: 3/4 — the policy analysis is the paper's strength; the funding and delivery mechanism needs more institutional realism before submission.

## What Works

The transponder tolling mechanism ($0.05/mile) is politically feasible in a way that traditional weight-mile trucking taxes are not. It uses existing ELD (Electronic Logging Device) infrastructure that all commercial trucks are already required to carry under the FMCSA ELD mandate, which means implementation cost is low (the transponder infrastructure is already deployed). The paper is right to identify this as the preferred funding mechanism.

The corridor selection methodology — 7 of 8 T1 corridors need managed lanes; I-40 exception at rural V/C<0.6 — demonstrates appropriate rigor. A program that identifies which corridors are not cost-effective is more credible than one that proposes managed lanes everywhere. The I-40 exception strengthens the paper's analytical credibility.

The best-to-worst B/C range (I-95 at 3.1:1 to I-90 at 1.6:1) provides a natural program phasing rationale: fund corridors in B/C order. Phase 1 (I-95, I-75 Atlanta, I-10 Gulf Coast) could proceed with strong economic justification while the lower-ranked corridors continue accumulating evidence.

The observation that all 8 corridors share a designation pattern (high V/C + high freight intensity on corridors already designated as NHFP Priority Freight Network segments) provides the congressional hook: the program could be framed as a managed-lane upgrade for the existing Priority Freight Network, which already has congressional authorization precedent.

## What Doesn't Work

The P3 feasibility assumption is underexplored. At $10–15M/lane-mile and a 2-lane per direction program, a 200-mile corridor requires roughly $4–6B in capital per corridor. This is at the upper end of the US P3 market — the largest US P3 highway projects (I-66 Outside, I-495 Express Lanes) are in the $1–3B range. A freight-only managed lane program on a 200-mile corridor at $4–6B would be the largest P3 highway project in US history. Whether private capital markets can absorb 7 such projects over a 20-year program horizon is an open question the paper does not address.

The paper's $121B total cost estimate should be compared against the IIJA total authorization ($1.2T, with ~$110B for roads, bridges, and major projects). At $121B, the managed freight lane program represents roughly 10% of total IIJA roads and bridges authorization — a program of this scale would require a dedicated authorization, not administrative discretion. The paper should acknowledge this and specify what legislative vehicle would be appropriate.

The transponder toll revenue ($2.3B/yr) takes approximately 30+ years to recover $121B at face value (not accounting for discount rate or inflation). The paper should show the full capital recovery schedule under the 7% discount rate used in the NPV analysis, and confirm whether the program is NPV-positive before toll revenue is included (suggesting the tolls are a revenue source, not the financial basis for feasibility) or only after (suggesting the program is dependent on toll revenue to achieve positive NPV).

## The Question I'd Push On

The ELD-based transponder system tracks truck location for hours-of-service compliance, not for road pricing. Expanding ELD to a per-mile pricing function requires either (a) statutory authorization of a new federal truck user fee (which would face organized resistance from ATA and state trucking associations) or (b) a voluntary access fee for the managed lanes (trucks that choose not to pay the fee use GP lanes instead).

If the tolling is voluntary — trucks pay $0.05/mile to access the managed freight lanes; trucks that don't pay continue on GP lanes — then the $2.3B/yr revenue projection depends entirely on how many trucks voluntarily choose the managed lane. What is the assumed uptake rate, and what premium service (guaranteed transit time, PTI 1.08) justifies the $0.05/mile fee for shippers? If the fee is mandatory for all trucks on these corridors (which requires separate congressional authority), the revenue projection is firmer but the political feasibility is lower. The paper should specify which model it assumes and model the demand response under the voluntary pricing scenario.
