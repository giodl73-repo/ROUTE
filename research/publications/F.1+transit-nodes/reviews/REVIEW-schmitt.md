## Overall

The paper's equity framing is more substantive than most freight-adjacent transit papers — the Gary south side and San Antonio south side examples are not decorative; they reflect genuine spatial alignment between hub locations and underserved communities. The C3 correlation (r=0.68) is a credible structural finding. But the paper conflates physical proximity with operational access in ways that Jarrett Walker's review will address in depth, and my concern is narrower: the paper's equity claims need to engage with the quality of bus service that transit-dependent riders would actually experience at T1/T1 hubs. An intercity bus platform in an exurban freight interchange is not the same as an urban bus station with amenities, safety features, and connections. The paper needs to specify what the $2.1M hub increment buys in terms of rider experience, not just infrastructure.

## What Works

The identification of specific transit-dependent communities within hub service areas (Gary south side, San Antonio south side, agricultural corridors) is the right approach to equity analysis — named communities, specific locations, not just aggregate statistics. This grounds the equity claim in geography rather than arithmetic.

The ACS B08201 data approach is appropriate as a first-order proxy for transit dependency, and the non-overlapping 30-mile catchment correction (preventing double-counting between adjacent hubs) is methodologically sound. The 12.4M figure is cited with enough methodological transparency that reviewers can assess it, even if the 30-mile radius is operationally problematic.

The $161/traveler-served vs. $2,200 standalone terminal comparison is a clean and defensible efficiency metric. Even if the effective traveler count is revised downward (as Walker's review will argue), the efficiency ratio likely survives — co-location is genuinely cheap relative to standalone construction.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper does not describe what the T1/T1 hub transit experience will actually be for a transit-dependent rider. A $2.1M investment on a freight interchange platform — passenger shelter, lighting, parking, scheduling information — is meaningfully different from a bus station designed around rider needs (indoor waiting, ticketing, restrooms, food service, safety features, ADA compliance, wayfinding). The paper should specify the design standard for the passenger increment and be honest about what $2.1M buys vs. what a rider-centered design would cost.

The pedestrian and cycling access to hub locations is not addressed. T1/T1 intersections are on managed freight lanes — by definition, high-speed limited-access corridors. Walking or cycling access is structurally excluded. This means the modal catchment for transit-dependent riders is local transit + park-and-ride only. Riders who cannot drive to the hub and cannot walk to the hub must rely on feeder service that the paper does not budget or plan.

The equity narrative for agricultural corridor hubs needs more specificity. Agricultural corridor workers — farmworkers, meatpacking workers — are among the highest transit-dependency and lowest vehicle-ownership populations in the country, but they are concentrated in specific corridors (San Joaquin Valley, Midwest meatpacking belt, Southeast poultry corridor) that may or may not align with T1/T1 hub locations. The paper should test this alignment explicitly rather than asserting it.

## The Question I'd Push On

The Gary south side is cited as an equity-positive hub location. Gary's south side residents are overwhelmingly Black, predominantly low-income, and currently underserved by both highway access and transit. A T1/T1 hub near Gary — on a managed freight lane on I-80/I-90 — would be accessible by car from suburban communities and inaccessible on foot or by local transit from Gary's residential neighborhoods, which have seen Gary Metro bus service cuts over the past decade. What is the specific local transit connection plan for the Gary hub, and who is responsible for funding it?
