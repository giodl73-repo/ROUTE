## Overall

The paper's rural equity argument is underdeveloped relative to its urban equity argument. T1/T1 hubs at major interstate intersections are, almost without exception, located at or near metropolitan areas — because T1-classified corridors are the highest-throughput national corridors, and those corridors are anchored by large cities. The rural agricultural communities the paper invokes are between the T1/T1 nodes, not at them. The T1/T2 regional stops (~50 locations) are where rural access lives, but the paper's hub-level analysis focuses on the 9 T1/T1 nodes. This is not a fatal flaw — the paper correctly identifies that 40+ new city pairs are connected — but the rural equity claim rests on T1/T2 stops that receive much less analytical attention than the T1/T1 hubs.

## What Works

The 40+ new city pair connections claim is the paper's strongest rural access finding. Many of these pairs will involve mid-size and smaller cities (Shreveport-Memphis, Amarillo-Albuquerque, Boise-Salt Lake City) that currently lack reliable intercity bus service. The paper should name and quantify the 10 most significant rural-serving new connections by current transit-dependent household count in the corridor's catchment.

The C3 dimension alignment finding (r=0.68) has a rural dimension that the paper underexploits. Some of the highest C3 scores in the ROUTE corpus are in corridors serving agricultural and energy-producing regions — these are also corridors where rural transit dependency is high but auto-dependence dominates because no transit exists. The paper should map which T1/T2 stops fall in these high-C3 rural corridors.

The agricultural corridor examples (referenced in the equity section) are the right geographic focus for rural analysis. Farmworker and meatpacking communities in these corridors have very high transit dependency and very low existing transit service.

## What Doesn't Work

The T1/T2 stops (~50 locations) receive almost no analytical treatment. For rural access, these stops are where the paper's equity claim lives, not the 9 T1/T1 hubs. A transit-dependent resident of Durant, Oklahoma or Effingham, Illinois is not served by a T1/T1 hub (those are in Dallas and Chicago, respectively). They are served, if at all, by a T1/T2 stop on I-35 or I-57. The paper should extend its accessibility analysis — catchment, transit-dependent population, local access availability — to the T1/T2 level.

The rural zero-vehicle household population is structurally different from the urban zero-vehicle population. Rural zero-vehicle households are disproportionately elderly (non-drivers who gave up driving) and low-income agricultural workers. For elderly rural non-drivers, intercity bus access requires either a local feeder (which rarely exists in rural areas) or a family/community car ride to the stop. The paper should acknowledge this access reality rather than implying that hub proximity equals access for rural populations.

The paper does not address the service frequency question for rural T1 corridors. A rural T1/T2 stop served by one bus per day in each direction has marginal utility for connecting to employment, healthcare, or family. The F.2 companion paper addresses scheduling in more detail, but F.1 should note that the equity value of hub access depends critically on service frequency, and rural corridors may have lower frequency than urban corridors due to lower ridership density.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper claims that agricultural corridors gain access through T1/T1 hub connectivity. But agricultural corridors are by definition between metropolitan areas — the T1/T1 hubs are in the metros. If a farmworker in the San Joaquin Valley needs to reach a T1/T1 hub in Los Angeles or Sacramento, they need either: (1) local transit to a T1/T2 stop on I-5, then a connecting bus to the T1/T1 hub; or (2) a car ride to the metro hub. Which of these pathways does the paper assume, and what is the probability that pathway (1) is operationally available in 2030?
