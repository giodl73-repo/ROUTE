## Overall

A well-constructed argument for treating port connectors as a distinct investment category, with a V/C analysis that I find credible at the corridor level. The paper's resilience coverage is uneven: the Savannah trajectory analysis is the best section in the paper, but it models demand growth without modeling climate exposure, which is a significant gap for a 5-year infrastructure projection. The I-710 climate analysis is minimal — correctly noting that Long Beach's 10–15ft elevation provides limited direct flood exposure — but misses the Port's own exposure to sea level rise and the connector's role as a chokepoint when the Port is disrupted. The paper does not need to be a climate paper, but for work proposing 30-year infrastructure investments in coastal locations, ignoring 2050 climate scenarios is a choice that reviewers will notice.

## What Works

The resilience failure mode in the connector taxonomy is correctly defined. The I-710's characteristic as a "single chokepoint with no alternate" is well-documented: POLA/POLB together handle 40% of US container imports, and there is no comparable alternate route if the I-710 is disrupted (I-405 and I-605 are not viable substitutes for heavy truck volumes). The paper correctly identifies this as a resilience failure distinct from a capacity failure, and the investment prescription (a redundant alternate connector) follows logically.

The scoring framework borrowing from the ROUTE corpus is appropriate. Using the ROUTE D1 (Freight Intensity) and D5 (Resilience) dimensions to contextualize port connector scores gives the paper an analytical anchor that ties it to the broader research program. The finding that the Houston Ship Channel connector scores D1=7.8 (extremely high freight intensity) while carrying a resilience score that the paper implies is low is the kind of dimensional tension the ROUTE framework is designed to surface.

The decision to present a time-bounded trajectory analysis for Savannah (2028 V/C=2.6 given 2020–2024 growth) rather than a single-point estimate reflects good practice for infrastructure planning under uncertainty. The paper could strengthen this by presenting a range (low/base/high CAGR scenarios), but the base-case projection is well-constructed.

## What Doesn't Work

**Climate exposure of connectors is treated as an afterthought.** The paper mentions that "I-10 Gulf Coast connector at Houston Ship Channel has D1=7.8" and that "Savannah is in a flood zone" but neither statement leads anywhere analytically. For the Houston case: the I-10 segment approaching the Houston Ship Channel sits at approximately 15–25ft elevation, but the connector approach roads and the port facility itself experienced major flooding in Harvey (2017) — the paper should cite TXDOT's post-Harvey infrastructure damage assessment and the subsequent investments in connector resilience. For Savannah: the I-16/I-95 interchange approach in Garden City is approximately 20–30ft above sea level, but the Savannah port facility and its drayage staging areas are near the Savannah River floodplain and have been designated as high-risk in FEMA's coastal flood maps updated post-2020. A 5-year trajectory to V/C=2.6 that does not account for even one major storm disruption in that window is projecting from a clean-signal baseline that reality will not provide.

**Sea level rise and the Port of Long Beach.** The paper's statement that I-710 has "minimal" climate exposure because Long Beach is "10–15ft above sea level" reflects a category error. The relevant exposure is not to the freeway segment — it is to the Port itself. POLB's wharf and container yard infrastructure is at 10–12ft elevation, within the range of projected sea level rise plus storm surge under IPCC AR6 high-emissions scenarios for 2050. If the Port must be progressively elevated or hardened — a process already underway in the Port's Climate Action Plan — the connector investment case changes: you are investing in a connector to a port that may need to restructure its own facility layout over the same investment horizon.

**No 2050 stress-test.** A 30-year infrastructure investment at the scale of $2–5B per connector should include a stress-test: "does this investment remain economically viable under 2050 climate and trade scenarios?" The paper proposes port connector standards without asking whether the ports themselves will be operating at current throughput in 2050. Gulf Coast ports face hurricane intensification risk; East Coast ports face sea level rise and storm surge risk; the Pacific Coast faces wildfire-driven supply chain disruption risk. None of this is modeled.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper presents the Savannah V/C=2.6 projection as a crisis requiring immediate investment decision. Given a 7–10 year investment lead time, the construction window is 2026–2036. Under NOAA's 2022 Sea Level Rise Technical Report, Savannah faces 0.5–1.5ft of sea level rise by 2050 under the intermediate scenario. What is the design-basis flood event for the proposed I-16/I-95 connector expansion, and is it consistent with FHWA's 2021 Climate Adaptation Guidance requiring consideration of 50-year and 100-year future climate conditions for major capital investments? If the connector design standard does not incorporate FHWA's climate adaptation requirements, the paper is proposing infrastructure that may not survive federal environmental review.
