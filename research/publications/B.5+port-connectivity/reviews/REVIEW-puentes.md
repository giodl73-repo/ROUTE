## Overall

A substantive and well-structured contribution. The empirical finding — that port connector segments are the binding freight capacity constraint for at least 5 major US ports, yet are excluded from T1-level analysis — is both defensible and policy-relevant. The paper's strongest move is to anchor its investment case to existing IIJA authorities and NHPP funding mechanisms, which grounds the proposal in what is administratively achievable. However, the paper significantly understates the complexity of the statutory change it proposes, and the jurisdictional analysis of the Laredo case is incomplete in ways that would undermine the proposal in any serious legislative or regulatory forum.

## What Works

The paper correctly identifies that the "port connector" classification proposal requires a change to 23 USC 103(c), which defines the categories of the National Highway System. The paper acknowledges this and is not misleading on the statutory path. The NHS amendment framing is the right framing — port connectors are distinct enough from T2/T3 (which are designed for regional access) to warrant a dedicated classification with its own design standards.

The NHPP funding eligibility discussion is accurate and useful. Port connectors are currently eligible for NHS-NHPP funding only where they are designated NHS segments — which most are not, precisely because they are not formally classified. The paper's proposed amendment would automatically bring qualifying connectors into NHPP eligibility, which is the right mechanism. This is a tighter argument than simply calling for "more port infrastructure funding."

The portfolio NPV framework is handled correctly. Using USDOT TIGER/INFRA benefit-cost methodology as the basis is appropriate for a paper targeting policy audiences, and the $2–5B/connector range, while wide, is accompanied by enough explanation of the variance drivers (land acquisition cost, existing right-of-way, urban vs. greenfield) to be useful.

## What Doesn't Work

**Legislative difficulty is understated.** The paper treats the 23 USC 103(c) amendment as a step in a regulatory process. It is not — it is primary legislation requiring a majority in both chambers and presidential signature. The last substantive NHS amendment was in the IIJA (2021), which required five years of negotiation and a $550B bipartisan package as the vehicle. A standalone amendment adding "port connector" as a new NHS category would need either (a) its own bipartisan coalition, or (b) attachment to the next surface transportation reauthorization (FAST Act expired 2021; IIJA expires 2026; MAP-21 successor timing is uncertain). The paper should map this legislative path explicitly, including which committee of jurisdiction (Senate EPW, House T&I) would need to move the amendment and what the likely reauthorization vehicle is.

**Laredo jurisdictional analysis is incomplete.** The USMCA border crossing connector at Laredo is controlled not by FHWA or TxDOT but by the General Services Administration (for the federal bridge) and US Customs and Border Protection (for the port-of-entry inspection facility). The connector from the POE gate to I-35 runs through the City of Laredo right-of-way on US-59/Saunders Avenue — a non-interstate, non-NHS segment. Any "port connector" classification at Laredo would require: (a) GSA/CBP agreement on operational modifications to the POE facility, (b) City of Laredo right-of-way cooperation, and (c) TxDOT coordination for the I-35 junction improvement. None of these agencies appear in the paper's Laredo section. The paper cannot recommend a "dedicated truck lane connector at Laredo" without addressing this multi-agency constraint.

**NHPP mechanism for non-NHS connectors.** The paper's proposal implicitly assumes that once a connector is classified as a "port connector" under the new 23 USC 103(c) category, it becomes NHS-NHPP eligible. But the enabling mechanism — the formula for apportioning NHPP funds and the states' obligation to include new designations in their NHS submissions — is not described. States control NHS designation within federal parameters; adding a new category does not automatically force states to designate qualifying connectors. The paper needs a sentence on the opt-in vs. opt-out question for state NHS submissions.

## The Question I'd Push On

The surface transportation reauthorization bill (the IIJA successor, expected 2026–2027) is the natural vehicle for the 23 USC 103(c) amendment. Which member of the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee or the House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee has a district interest in port connector investment that would motivate them to champion this amendment? The paper avoids naming political champions — understandably, for an academic journal — but should at minimum map the district-level concentration of port connector investment benefits to identify where the legislative coalition would come from. This is not a gap that prevents publication, but a reviewer at *Maritime Policy and Management* will expect the paper to demonstrate familiarity with how US transportation legislation actually moves.
