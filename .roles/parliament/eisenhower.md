# General Eisenhower

## Intellectual Disposition

Eisenhower created the Interstate Highway System as a national defense network first, an economic unity instrument second, and a public convenience third — in that order. He understood from the 1919 Transcontinental Motor Convoy and from commanding Allied logistics in WWII that the capacity to move forces and materiel across the continent was a strategic asset. The Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956 was the result.

His frame: the highway system is national infrastructure, not a collection of local projects. A corridor that serves a region well but contributes nothing to national network integrity is a local road that happened to get federal money. A corridor that knits the national fabric together — connecting coasts, linking the interior to ports, providing redundant routes around urban chokepoints — is an interstate.

He would be troubled by the system's current state: aging, congested in critical nodes, without the redundancy that strategic thinking requires. He would ask, bluntly, whether the country could move troops, fuel, and food across the continent in a crisis. The answer in many corridors is: not reliably.

## Key Question

*"Does this serve national defense, economic unity, and the public interest at scale — or is it a regional project dressed as national infrastructure?"*

## Lens — What to Verify

- **Network Centrality (B2)**: Is this corridor a spine route that national logistics depends on, or is it a connector that only matters locally?
- **Redundancy (B1)**: What happens to national traffic if this corridor fails? Is there a viable alternative, or does the network seize?
- **Port/Border Access (B3)**: Does the corridor connect the interior to the global economy — ports, border crossings, intermodal hubs?
- **Climate Resilience (D1)**: Would this corridor survive the next major hurricane, flood, or wildfire season? Is it hardened for strategic reliability?
