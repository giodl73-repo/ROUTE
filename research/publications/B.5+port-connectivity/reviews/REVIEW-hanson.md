## Overall

The paper makes a legitimate and important empirical contribution: port connector segments are the binding freight capacity constraint for much of the US port system, and they are invisible in standard tier-based analysis. I do not dispute this finding. What I dispute is the paper's decision to frame a freight capacity and investment-sequencing argument while running a freeway expansion proposal through communities that are among the most pollution-burdened in the United States — and then treating those communities as invisible. The I-710 Long Beach Freeway runs through Wilmington and West Long Beach, communities that CalEnviroScreen 4.0 ranks in the 95th–99th percentile for cumulative environmental burden. A paper recommending widening or upgrading the I-710 that does not address this environmental justice dimension is not publishable in any venue that takes social impact seriously, and *Maritime Policy and Management* does.

## What Works

The failure-mode taxonomy is genuinely useful for transport geography. The distinction between capacity failure, classification failure, resilience failure, and jurisdictional fragmentation maps onto real governance and planning categories that planners actually work with. Port planning at POLA/POLB has operated with exactly this kind of fragmentation — four agencies with overlapping jurisdiction and no coordinating authority — and the paper names this correctly.

The spatial analysis of which ports have the worst connectors, while not presented in full detail, provides a basis for geographic prioritization that could be extended to account for community impact. The paper at least establishes which corridors are worst in capacity terms, which is a prerequisite for the equity analysis it does not perform.

The Savannah case study is well-chosen. The I-16/I-95 connector serves a port that has grown faster than any other on the East Coast, and the paper's projection of V/C=2.6 by 2028 is plausible. Importantly, the communities adjacent to this corridor are not subject to the same degree of cumulative burden as those along I-710, so the equity complexity is lower — which makes Savannah a more straightforward investment case than LA/Long Beach. The paper could exploit this contrast more explicitly.

## What Doesn't Work

**The I-710 community impact is entirely absent.** The I-710 Long Beach Freeway is the paper's flagship case — highest NPV, highest existing V/C, 40% of US container imports. It is also one of the most intensively studied environmental justice corridors in the United States. CalEnviroScreen 4.0 data for census tracts in Wilmington (90744), West Long Beach (90810), and Compton (90220) show PM2.5, diesel PM, traffic, and asthma burden in the 95th percentile or higher. The Southern California Association of Governments (SCAG) 2024 Regional Transportation Plan includes an explicit environmental justice analysis of the I-710 corridor. CalSTA's 2023 Freight Corridor Environmental Justice Study is publicly available. The paper cites none of these. A recommendation to invest $2–5B in I-710 capacity expansion without acknowledging the decades-long community opposition and the existing EJ analysis is not a research oversight — it is a choice to treat those communities as unimportant. That choice is not defensible.

**The geographic pattern of connector-adjacent communities is undescribed.** The paper identifies which ports have the worst connectors but does not examine the demographic pattern of communities adjacent to those connectors. This is a straightforward spatial analysis: buffer each connector segment by 0.5 miles, intersect with census tract demographics and CalEnviroScreen/EPA EJScreen data, and report mean pollution burden percentile for each connector. My expectation — based on the history of US urban freeway siting — is that the worst connectors disproportionately run through Black, Latino, and low-income communities. If true, this is a significant finding that should be reported. If false, that is also worth reporting. The paper has no grounds to omit this analysis.

**Widening vs. alternatives.** The paper defaults to capacity expansion (additional lanes, managed lanes) as the investment prescription without considering whether operational improvements — truck appointment systems, off-peak gate incentives, off-dock container staging — could relieve V/C without widening. POLA/POLB already operate a truck appointment system (DTSC Turn Time Program). The paper should engage with the evidence on whether appointment systems reduce peak V/C sufficiently to defer or eliminate the need for physical expansion. At least for I-710, where community opposition to physical widening is intense and well-organized, an operational-improvement-first strategy may be the only politically viable path.

## The Question I'd Push On

If the I-710 upgrade proceeds at the scale the paper recommends, what is the community benefit agreement framework? The existing ICTF/I-710 EIR process has produced community benefit agreements addressing diesel truck replacement, local hiring, and air quality mitigation. The paper should at minimum cite these precedents and recommend that any "port connector" investment standard under the proposed 23 USC 103(c) amendment include a community benefit agreement requirement as a condition of NHPP funding eligibility. This would not weaken the freight investment case; it would make the proposal politically viable, because without EJ mitigation requirements, I-710 expansion cannot move through California's environmental review process.
