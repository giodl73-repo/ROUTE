## Overall

The Essential Intercity Transportation (EIT) designation is the paper's central policy mechanism for ensuring that underserved T1 corridors receive operating subsidy. As I noted in my F.1 review, this designation does not exist in federal law. In F.2, the problem is more acute because the paper designs an entire subsidy architecture around it — the $15/passenger subsidy cap, the "underserved route" designation criteria, and the funding authorization are all predicated on EIT authority that Congress has not granted. The paper's policy recommendations are therefore partially non-operational. This is a P1 issue. On the freight-bus access question (managed lane allocation for bus operators), the paper is right to identify this as an unresolved mechanism but does not provide enough detail on how the allocation would work in practice.

## What Works

The PTI 1.15 → timetable scheduling → higher load factor → lower operator cost chain is the paper's cleanest policy logic. Identifying that managed lane reliability (PTI 1.15) is the precondition for viable timetabled intercity bus operations — rather than a nice-to-have — is a genuine operational insight. This is the right way to connect infrastructure investment to bus operator economics, and it is argued clearly enough for policymakers to follow.

The $3.1B annual revenue estimate at market equilibrium is appropriately framed as an upper bound contingent on the 24M passenger figure (which has its own uncertainty, as Neumark will note). The paper is transparent enough about this dependency that the revenue figure reads as an illustration rather than a projection, which is the appropriate framing at this stage.

The Amtrak competitive positioning is useful framing for a federal policy audience. The finding that T1 bus is faster on 11 of 12 corridors compared to current Amtrak service — except Memphis-Chicago where Amtrak is close — will resonate with Congressional audiences frustrated by Amtrak's limited national reach. This comparison should be made more visually prominent (a table showing T1 bus vs. Amtrak vs. driving by corridor).

## What Doesn't Work

The Essential Intercity Transportation designation is invoked without statutory grounding. The analogy to EAS (Essential Air Service, 49 U.S.C. § 41731) is instructive — EAS subsidizes air service to communities that would otherwise lose access following deregulation — but it required specific statutory creation and dedicated funding authorization. Creating EIT for intercity bus would require, at minimum: (a) a statutory definition of "essential intercity transportation" and "underserved community" for bus purposes; (b) a federal agency designation authority (probably USDOT); (c) funding authorization (probably through FAST Act or surface transportation reauthorization successor); (d) operator eligibility criteria. None of this is described. The paper should either walk through the legislative pathway or replace EIT with existing DOT programs (FTA 5311, Intercity Bus portion, which already funds rural intercity bus service under 49 U.S.C. § 5311(f)).

The managed lane access allocation mechanism is correctly identified as a gap ("bus operator access is not guaranteed and the paper needs to address the allocation mechanism") but the paper only flags the issue rather than resolving it. This is a design decision with major policy implications: if freight carriers have exclusive access to managed lanes and bus operators must negotiate access, the reliability benefits of managed lanes for bus operations depend on carrier goodwill or a regulatory framework that does not currently exist. The paper should at minimum propose an allocation model (time-of-day access windows, speed-differentiated access rights, auction-based access) and identify the regulatory authority that would implement it.

## The Question I'd Push On

FTA Section 5311(f) currently requires that at least 15% of a state's 5311 apportionment be spent on intercity bus service. In 2023, this produced approximately $390M nationally for rural intercity bus. If T1 corridors serve rural communities as the paper claims, how much of the 12-corridor T1 bus network could be funded through existing 5311(f) authority — and how much would require the new EIT designation the paper proposes? A funding gap analysis using existing vs. proposed authority would clarify how much new legislation is actually required.
