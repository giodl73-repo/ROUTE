## Overall

The paper makes a structurally important contribution: it identifies a mechanism (freight hub co-location) by which high-quality intercity bus access can be created at marginal cost on a freight investment. The $161/traveler figure (vs. $2,200 for a standalone terminal) is the right number to lead with — it is the paper's cleanest finding. But the central equity and ridership claim — 12.4 million transit-dependent Americans within 30 miles of a hub — is built on a proximity definition that is not operational. Proximity is not access. A transit-dependent traveler in Gary's south side cannot walk 30 miles to a T1/T1 hub. They need local transit to get there. The paper's entire demand and equity architecture depends on the assumption that physical proximity translates into effective access, and this assumption is nowhere tested or defended. This is my coverage vs. ridership critique applied to the intercity scale: you can draw a 30-mile circle around every hub in the country and claim coverage of millions; none of it matters if there is no local transit connection.

## What Works

The co-location efficiency argument is sound and important. T1/T1 freight hub infrastructure — drainage, access roads, parking, lighting, utilities — is already committed capital. Adding a passenger platform, shelter, and scheduling information at $2.1M per hub is a genuinely marginal cost, and the $161/traveler-served figure reflects this correctly if the traveler denominator is sound (which is my central concern). The paper should lead harder with this mechanism: the value proposition is not that T1/T1 hubs are transit hubs, it is that they happen to be located where intercity freight and intercity passengers have overlapping demand catchments, and the shared infrastructure cost is almost zero at the margin.

The C3 alignment finding (r=0.68 between economic opportunity score and transit-dependent household density at hub locations) is the paper's second-strongest result. It suggests the hub location methodology — driven by freight rubric scores — is not randomly distributed relative to equity need. This is a meaningful structural observation even if the correlation does not prove access.

The 40+ new city pair connections claim is verifiable and the paper should make it more concrete by listing the 10 most significant new connections by current unmet demand.

## What Doesn't Work

The "within 30 miles" catchment is operationally meaningless for transit-dependent travelers. A transit-dependent household — by definition — lacks access to a private automobile. Reaching a T1/T1 hub 30 miles from their home requires local transit service. The paper needs to answer: for each of the 9 T1/T1 hubs, what local transit services connect the hub to the surrounding population? If the hub is on a managed freight lane outside the urban core (which most T1/T1 intersections are, by design), local transit access may be zero or minimal. The 12.4M figure could collapse to 1-2M effectively reachable travelers once local transit access is accounted for.

The 12.4M figure uses ACS B08201 (households without vehicles) as a proxy for transit dependency. This is a reasonable first-order proxy but it conflates two populations: (1) urban zero-vehicle households with local transit access to the hub, and (2) rural zero-vehicle households with no transit access to the hub at all. These populations have opposite implications for hub utility. The paper should separate them.

The $2.1M per hub increment cost does not appear to include the cost of local access improvements (transit shuttle service, bike/ped connections, feeder bus routes) needed to make the hub functionally accessible to transit-dependent travelers. If $2.1M is construction cost only, the effective cost per reachable transit-dependent traveler is much higher than $161.

## The Question I'd Push On

For each of the 9 T1/T1 hub locations, what is the local transit service within 1 mile of the hub (bus routes, frequency, operating hours)? I would accept a simple table: hub name, nearest local transit stop, distance to transit stop, frequency of service, and operating hours. If the answer for more than 4 of the 9 hubs is "no local transit within 1 mile," the 12.4M figure requires a substantial downward revision and the paper's equity claims must be scoped accordingly.
