## Overall

This is a well-constructed and empirically grounded contribution to a question that federal transportation planners have largely avoided asking rigorously: where exactly are the access gaps in the interstate network, and what would it cost to close them? The 30-mile standard is defensible, the four-zone taxonomy is analytically coherent, and the coverage efficiency metric gives practitioners a first-pass prioritization tool. My primary concerns are about the disconnect between what the paper identifies and what the paper proposes to build. Twelve corridors totaling $292B is a wish list; the paper would be stronger — and more immediately actionable for a TRR audience — if it engaged seriously with the IIJA funding architecture and the political economy of new corridor designation.

## What Works

The four-zone gap taxonomy (Northern Tier, Appalachians, Gulf South, Rural West) is the paper's most durable policy contribution. This classification is immediately useful to MPOs, state DOTs, and congressional delegations because it provides a geographic framing attachable to appropriations requests. I-3 and the Northern Tier corridor already appear in various state LRTPs; this paper provides the national analytical foundation those plans currently lack.

The phased investment structure ($71B Phase 1 / $60B Phase 2 / $161B Phase 3) is a meaningful improvement over presenting the full $292B as a single undifferentiated ask. This is the kind of phasing structure that a congressional authorizing committee can work with. Phase 1 roughly tracks the scale of a single IIJA-tier surface transportation authorization.

The C3 economic opportunity correlation — gap counties scoring 41% higher — is the finding that should lead this paper. It reframes the investment case from "rural charity" to "economic integration": these are not populations being subsidized, they are populations being denied participation in the national economic network. This should be in the abstract, the introduction, and the conclusion. It is currently buried in Section 5.

## What Doesn't Work

**Budget contextualization is insufficient.** $292B is presented without grounding in existing federal highway funding levels. The IIJA allocated approximately $110B for roads and bridges over five years. Phase 1 alone ($71B) equals more than three full years of the entire federal road budget. Phase 3 alone ($161B) exceeds the entire IIJA road allocation. The paper should state this explicitly: the full 12-corridor program is a 25–30 year program at current federal investment rates, not a single authorization cycle commitment. Without this framing, the budget section reads as either naive or deliberately evasive.

**Political feasibility of corridor designation is absent.** New interstate designations have been extraordinarily rare since 1982 — the Interstate designation process requires congressional authorization, FHWA approval, and state agreements. The Northern Tier corridor (6 state borders, 1,800+ miles, minimal existing right-of-way) is categorically different from I-69 completion (30+ years of authorization history, active state programs in TX and IN, partial construction complete). Ranking these by coverage efficiency without acknowledging the feasibility gap misrepresents the investment horizon. A "barriers to implementation" sidebar or table would prevent the paper from being dismissed by practitioners.

**NEPA timelines are invisible.** A new interstate corridor requires 10–20 years from authorization to construction start under current federal environmental review requirements. The 2040 national coverage target is incompatible with this timeline for any corridor requiring new right-of-way. The paper should either acknowledge this and shift the target date, or argue explicitly for NEPA reform as a prerequisite — which is itself a significant policy position.

## The Question I'd Push On

If you had to identify the single corridor where the combination of coverage gap served, political alignment, existing partial construction, and available IIJA funding mechanisms makes near-term progress most likely — not the most efficient corridor by your metric, but the most *buildable* one within one authorization cycle — which would it be? I-69 completion (Texarkana to Indianapolis) has been authorized since 1991, has active programs in multiple states, and has received IIJA funding. It ranks 4th in your efficiency metric. Why isn't it presented as the "Phase 1A" recommendation with the others deferred? A paper that tells a congressional transportation committee "here is the next corridor that can actually be built and funded" will move the field farther than one that presents a 30-year optimal portfolio.
