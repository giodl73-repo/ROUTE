## Overall
The paper's economics are sound and the matching formulation is rigorous. My concerns are geographic: the analysis is fundamentally a national average applied to a system with extreme spatial heterogeneity, and the geographic distribution of empty-mile reduction benefits is not adequately developed. The corridor flow imbalance table (Table 1) is the paper's most geographically sophisticated contribution, but it is only six corridors. The paper claims to rank the top 20 T1/T2 corridors (Introduction, Section 1.4) but Table 3 shows only 10. The spatial analysis needs to be more complete, particularly for the rural corridor question.

## What Works

**The corridor flow imbalance table is the paper's most important geographic contribution.** Table 1's outbound/inbound ratios for I-29 (1.8), I-10 (0.6), I-95 (1.4) identify the structural geographic drivers of empty miles — where commodity flows are systematically imbalanced, empty backhaul rates are structurally elevated above the national average. This is the correct geographic framing: empty miles are not uniformly distributed across the network, and the relay hub's value is geographically concentrated.

**The Fargo hub analysis is the right counterexample.** The paper correctly identifies that I-29 near Fargo operates in a structurally different regime from I-80 near Chicago — lower truck volume, agricultural outbound surplus, and load availability from a smaller catchment. The analysis shows the relay hub adds value in the Fargo context by surfacing available return loads rather than by selecting the best match from a rich choice set. This geographic specificity is exactly what's missing from the rest of the paper.

**The top-10 corridor table (Table 3) is a useful spatial prioritization tool.** The ranking by annual efficiency gain provides a geographic investment sequencing argument — build relay hubs on I-80, I-95, and I-10 first, then extend to lower-volume corridors. This is operationally valuable even if the spatial analysis needs deepening.

**The carbon accounting is appropriately corridor-level.** Linking 45 billion fewer empty miles to the 5.8 MMT CO2 reduction and identifying that the gains are disproportionately concentrated on high-volume corridors (I-80, I-95) is the right geographic carbon analysis.

## What Doesn't Work

**Rural corridors have higher empty backhaul rates and are underserved by relay hub matching.** The paper's introduction notes that "rural corridors have higher empty backhaul rates than urban." This is correct — agricultural outbound corridors (I-29, I-90 west of Bismarck, I-82 in the Yakima valley) have structural empty rates above 40% on the inbound leg because there is nothing to pick up going back. But the relay hub load-matching function depends on load density within a 400-mile catchment — and rural catchments have lower load density than the Chicago or Atlanta metropolitan areas. The paper should explicitly quantify the mismatch between where empty miles are worst (rural agricultural corridors) and where the relay hub platform works best (high-density urban corridor hubs). This is not a fatal flaw in the platform design, but it is a limitation that needs to be stated.

**The claim to rank the top 20 T1/T2 corridors is not fulfilled.** The introduction (Section 1.4) states "we estimate the national empty-mile reduction achievable through relay hub deployment on the top 20 T1/T2 corridors." Table 3 shows only 10 corridors. The other 10 are described as "the long tail of regional and secondary corridors" generating $72B of the $135B total — but they are not named or analyzed. Either provide the full 20-corridor table or revise the claim to match the analysis presented.

**The flow imbalance map is absent.** Section 2.1 presents flow imbalance data for six corridors in table form. A map showing corridor-level empty backhaul rates and relay hub pre-matching potential would be the paper's most spatially informative contribution — and it is missing. Transportation Research Part E routinely publishes corridor-level freight flow maps; the absence of one here is notable. The geographic distribution of the $135B efficiency gain is a natural candidate for a map.

## The Question I'd Push On
For rural agricultural corridors (I-29 from Fargo to Kansas City, I-90 west of Bismarck, I-82 in Washington state) where empty backhaul rates exceed 40% on the inbound leg, what is the realistic load-matching potential of a relay hub given low catchment load density — and is the relay hub's value in these corridors primarily about driver welfare (relay drivers can work agricultural outbound corridor legs without being away from home) rather than empty-mile reduction?
