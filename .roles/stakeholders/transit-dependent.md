# Transit-Dependent Traveler

## Who this is

A person who cannot or does not drive — elderly, disabled, low-income, or by choice — who depends on intercity bus, coach, or connecting transit to travel between communities. May be a daily commuter, a student, a healthcare patient, or a worker. The highway system's relationship to transit determines whether this person can travel at all.

## Primary concerns

**Intercity coach access**: Greyhound, FlixBus, and regional carriers operate almost exclusively on interstates. Stops are at or near interchange areas. A corridor with no bus service is a corridor that transit-dependent travelers cannot use. A corridor with bus service but no safe waiting area, no restroom, and no shelter is one they use at risk.

**Shared transit facilities**: A park-and-ride or transit hub at a rural interchange — with weather protection, lighting, restrooms, route information, and safe vehicle access — is a transformative investment for rural transit-dependent travelers. These facilities cost a fraction of a lane-mile and serve users who have no alternative.

**First and last mile**: Intercity bus drops travelers at an interchange or a downtown bus terminal. The distance from that stop to the actual destination — and the availability of local transit, rideshare, or pedestrian infrastructure to cover it — determines whether the trip is actually completable.

**Frequency and reliability**: A bus that comes once a day on a route means a missed connection is a day's delay. Transit-dependent travelers have less tolerance for unreliability because they have fewer alternatives.

## What ROUTE should capture from this stakeholder

- Intercity bus service presence on the corridor (routes and stop locations)
- Transit facility quality at interchanges (shelter, lighting, restrooms, information)
- Park-and-ride availability at corridor interchanges
- First/last-mile connection options at major corridor stops

## Lens for scoring

The transit-dependent traveler grounds D2 (Multimodal Integration) in the reality of non-driving access. A corridor that scores high on D2 should have actual transit infrastructure — not just rail tracks running parallel — that a person without a car can use to travel along the corridor.
