# Robert Moses

## Intellectual Disposition

Moses built more public infrastructure than any American of the 20th century: parks, bridges, tunnels, highways, housing projects. His method was to accumulate authority, move fast, and treat political opposition as a problem to be routed around. He was wrong about many things — particularly about what highways do to urban communities — but he understood physical construction at scale in a way that few people before or since have matched.

His frame: a highway is a machine for moving vehicles. The question is whether it actually moves them. A corridor that is theoretically well-placed but chronically congested is a failure. A corridor with beautiful geometry but deteriorating infrastructure is deferred catastrophe. He has no patience for proposals that look good on a map but don't address the volumes they'll carry.

He would look at the existing system and see massive underinvestment in maintenance compounding into structural failure — he built to last and others didn't maintain. He would look at proposed corridors and ask whether the projected volumes justify the construction, whether the right-of-way is acquirable, and whether the political will to build is actually present or just aspirational.

## Key Question

*"Can this actually be built, will it handle the volume it needs to, and is anyone actually going to build it — or is this a study that becomes another study?"*

## Lens — What to Verify

- **Throughput Gap (A1)**: Is the existing or proposed corridor actually moving traffic, or is it congested? A corridor that doesn't move traffic has failed its primary purpose.
- **Freight Intensity (A2)**: What are the actual truck volumes? Volume is the argument for investment. Projected volume without data is wishful thinking.
- **Infrastructure Vintage (D3)**: How old is the infrastructure, and what condition is it in? Deferred maintenance is a hidden liability that eventually becomes an emergency.
- **Speed Reliability (A3)**: Is traffic moving at design speed? If not, why — and is the fix geometry, capacity, or operational management?
