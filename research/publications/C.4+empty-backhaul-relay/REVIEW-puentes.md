## Overall
The paper's economics are strong and the relay terminal as a load-matching platform is a genuinely useful policy insight. The policy section (Section 7) is the weakest part, as it raises the FMCSA data sharing requirement and the relay terminal designation questions without resolving either. These are not merely technical questions — they are the binding policy constraints for whether the relay hub load-matching function is operationally achievable. The paper needs to address them directly or its policy contribution is incomplete.

## What Works

**The relay terminal designation as prerequisite is correctly identified.** Section 7 notes that hub operators need data access (truck arrival windows, load availability, driver HOS clocks) that requires regulatory authority to mandate. This is the right policy insight: the load-matching platform's information advantage is only achievable if FMCSA or DOT requires carriers to share scheduling data with hub operators. The paper correctly identifies this as a prerequisite rather than a voluntary add-on.

**The competition with existing broker platforms is addressed.** Section 2.4 correctly observes that the relay hub is not a direct competitor to Uber Freight and Convoy because it operates at a different information timescale (4–8 hours advance vs. reactive). The paper frames the hub as complementary to existing broker infrastructure, which is the right framing for a policy paper: regulators will not adopt a system that appears to disintermediate existing private markets without justification.

**The carbon and capacity co-benefits are appropriately quantified.** The 5.8 MMT CO2/year reduction and 1.3 million passenger-car equivalent is a useful comparison point for transportation policy audiences. The derivation from 45 billion fewer empty truck-miles per year is transparent.

**The managed-lane capital cost comparison is the paper's strongest policy finding.** Demonstrating that load-matching generates $113–135B/year — exceeding the $121B managed-lane capex — is the kind of concrete benefit-cost comparison that moves from academic publication to policy consideration. This should be in the abstract.

## What Doesn't Work

**The FMCSA data sharing requirement is raised but not analyzed.** Section 7 notes that carriers would need to share load availability data with hub operators and that this is "a major privacy/competitive concern." But it does not address: (a) whether FMCSA has existing authority under 49 CFR Part 390 or other safety regulations to require ELD data sharing with third-party hub operators; (b) whether a data sharing requirement would survive First Amendment (compelled speech on commercial data) or Fourth Amendment (warrantless access to business records) challenge; (c) what the minimum data sharing requirement is to enable pre-matching — does the hub need carrier identity, or only anonymized truck arrival time and trailer type? This is not a minor implementation detail; it is the legal prerequisite for the entire platform.

**The relay terminal designation is assumed rather than derived.** Section 7 states that relay terminal designation is "the prerequisite" for hub operators to access the data needed for pre-matching. But who designates relay terminals? Under what authority? The paper references FHWA's National Highway Freight Program authority but does not specify the designation mechanism, the approval process, or the timeline. If relay terminal designation requires a NEPA review, the three-to-five year permitting timeline could delay the information-architecture benefits considerably.

**The competition with broker platforms is underanalyzed.** The paper notes that relay hub matching "does not compete with existing broker platforms" because of the different information timescale. But carriers who use the relay hub pre-matching service will be less likely to call a spot broker after hub departure — because they already have a load. The relay hub will capture load-matching revenue (or deliver cost savings) that currently flows to broker commissions ($375–500 per load × 200,000 loads/day = $27–37B/year in broker revenue). The paper should address whether this is an intended policy consequence and how existing broker platforms are likely to respond.

## The Question I'd Push On
Does FMCSA have existing regulatory authority to require carriers to share load scheduling data with relay terminal operators — or does enabling relay hub pre-matching require new legislation? If new legislation is required, what is the minimum statutory change needed, and has any member of Congress introduced analogous freight data sharing legislation that could serve as a vehicle?
