## Overall
The paper describes a $8.2B/year economic opportunity and a 20,000-job labor market innovation without once asking who captures the $8.2B, whether the 20,000 relay jobs are good jobs, or what happens to the communities and workers displaced by the freight network reconfiguration. This is not a minor methodological gap — it is a missing analytical dimension for a JEP paper claiming to quantify the economic opportunities unlocked by I2.0. The paper as written describes benefits flowing overwhelmingly to large shippers and coastal consumers while locating new employment in the interior; the distributional analysis would likely show mixed results that would strengthen, not weaken, the paper's credibility.

## What Works

**The relay driver as labor market innovation is genuinely important.** The analysis in Section 6 that the driver shortage is a lifestyle problem, not a wage problem, is the paper's most socially important finding. The labor pool expansion argument — that parents, caregivers, and workers with medical conditions excluded from long-haul trucking can participate in relay driving — is well-argued and addresses a real equity gap in the current freight workforce. This section deserves to be more prominent, not buried after the shipper-focused economic cases.

**The geographic distribution of relay jobs is noted correctly.** Section 6 observes that the $8.2B in air substitution savings flows to coastal shippers while the 80,000 relay network jobs are distributed in the interior — Cleveland, Omaha, Salt Lake City. This is the paper's implicit equity argument and it is correct. It should be made explicitly, not as a footnote.

**The health section is excellent and underused.** The analysis of long-haul trucker health outcomes — elevated obesity, hypertension, cardiovascular disease — is strong public health economics. A career that burns out workers in ten years has real social costs that the paper identifies but does not quantify. The relay model's health improvement is a benefit that belongs in the economic accounting, even if rough.

**The relay station as community anchor framing is right.** The observation that a 200-person employer (50 drivers + 150 support) at an interior interstate interchange is a meaningful economic anchor for rural and small-town communities is both true and important for the political economy of I2.0 support.

## What Doesn't Work

**The $8.2B savings: who gets it?** Section 3 calculates $8.2B in freight cost savings and notes it is "a logistics cost transfer, not a GDP addition." But it never follows this cost transfer to its beneficiaries. Shippers who currently pay air freight rates include both large corporations (Amazon, pharmaceutical distributors) and small California agricultural producers. The distributional profile of who saves $8.2B matters enormously for evaluating whether this is a pro-equity or anti-equity investment. A pharmaceutical distributor saving $2B per year in air freight costs is not the same as a small strawberry grower gaining market access to New York.

**The relay driver jobs: are these good jobs?** Section 6 states relay drivers earn $58,000/year and frames this as "regional premium; no overnight." But it does not address: union status, benefits (health insurance, retirement), job security (gig vs. W-2), scheduling stability, or career progression. $58,000/year without benefits and with variable scheduling is a worse deal than $65,000/year with union health insurance and seniority protections. The paper's labor case requires a more complete picture of job quality, not just wage level.

**Displacement of Greyhound and intercity bus workers is not addressed.** Section 1's introduction describes the relay hub at the T1 diamond interchange, which is also described in F.1 and F.2 as an intercity transit node. If freight relay displaces existing bus terminal workers or changes the labor mix at existing transport hubs, that displacement should be analyzed. More broadly, any large freight infrastructure investment reorganizes freight networks in ways that benefit some corridors and disadvantage others; the paper does not acknowledge the potential negative effects on workers in corridors not served by I2.0.

## The Question I'd Push On
Of the $8.2B per year in air-to-truck freight cost savings, what share accrues to large corporate shippers versus small agricultural producers versus retail consumers through lower prices — and what does the distributional map look like? Without this, the paper's economic case cannot be evaluated for equity impact.
