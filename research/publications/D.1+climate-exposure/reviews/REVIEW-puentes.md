## What Works

The PROTECT program critique is the paper's strongest policy contribution. The program, authorized under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA), allocates formula funds based on NHS mileage within FEMA SFHA zones. If total mileage is the allocation variable and consecutive mileage is the risk variable, then the allocation is systematically mismatched. The paper demonstrates this mismatch with specific corridor data.

The policy implication is clear: Louisiana receives less PROTECT formula funding per unit of operational closure risk than Texas or Florida. This is not an abstract concern — Louisiana DOTD has been requesting federal support for I-10 elevation and drainage improvements in coastal parishes since the 2005 Katrina aftermath, and the federal allocation formula has not reflected the severity of the problem.

The priority order finding — Gulf Coast I-10 becomes the most critical climate adaptation investment by 2050 — is the kind of forward-looking statement that congressional appropriators need. Short-term allocation formulas tend to reflect current conditions; the paper's 2050 projection (even with its methodological limitations) argues for locking in priority rankings now before conditions shift further.

The venue choice (Nature Climate Change) is appropriate for reaching the science-policy audience that can translate this into PROTECT rulemaking comments or GAO review requests.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper does not translate its D1 metric into dollar terms for the PROTECT allocation. The reader is left to infer that a higher D1 score should correspond to higher PROTECT funding, but the paper does not show what the funding shift would be under a consecutive-mile formula versus the current total-mile formula, nor does it quantify the allocation gap for Louisiana.

This is a significant omission for a policy paper. If PROTECT formula funds are $1.4B/year (the IIJA authorization level), and Louisiana currently receives X% while a consecutive-mile formula would give it Y%, that delta is the finding. Without it, "systematically underweights" is a qualitative claim, not a quantitative one.

The paper also does not engage with the institutional mechanics of formula change. PROTECT funds are allocated by formula in statute; changing the formula requires either a new rule (which is within FHWA's discretion if the current formula is regulatory, not statutory) or new legislation. The paper should at minimum identify which path is applicable and what the barriers are. Researchers who have gone through this exercise before know that FHWA formula changes can take 3–7 years through rulemaking; a 2050 projection without acknowledgment of that implementation timeline is incomplete policy analysis.

The winter corridor comparison (Donner, Snoqualmie) creates a subtle problem for the PROTECT framing. PROTECT funds are specifically designated for climate resilience; winter closures due to snow are classified as a climate hazard, but one that falls under a different FHWA program category than coastal flooding. The paper implicitly argues for a unified framework, but does not engage with the statutory boundary between PROTECT (coastal/inland flood focus) and other NHPP set-asides (bridge condition, safety).

## The Question I'd Push On

PROTECT grants have both formula and discretionary components. The formula component is allocated by state; the discretionary component is project-competitive. Has the Louisiana DOTD submitted PROTECT discretionary grant applications for the I-10 coastal corridor segments? If so, what was the outcome?

If Louisiana has applied and been rejected, or if the corridor has not been funded through the discretionary component despite its D1 score, that is a more damning finding than the formula critique alone — it suggests the scoring criteria for discretionary awards are also misaligned with operational risk. If Louisiana has not applied, the paper's critique of federal allocation may be partly a critique of state grant-seeking capacity, which is a different problem with different solutions. The paper should engage with this question rather than treating the allocation gap as solely a federal formula problem.
