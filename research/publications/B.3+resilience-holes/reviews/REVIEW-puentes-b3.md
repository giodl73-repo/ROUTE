## Overall

This paper makes a strong and needed case for incorporating network topology (B1 isolation) into federal resilience investment criteria. The PROTECT program reform recommendation is the paper's most immediately actionable contribution, and it is the right recommendation: current PROTECT grant criteria are effectively D1-only, and this misallocates federal resilience investment toward corridors that close frequently but reroute easily, while underinvesting in corridors that close less often but produce national freight disruption when they do. The compound score criterion would be a genuine improvement. My concerns are about the specificity of the PROTECT reform proposal and the political feasibility of the National Critical Highway Infrastructure designation. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The compound exposure concept fills a real gap in the federal resilience policy framework. The Section 6.2 analysis — that PROTECT grant criteria are D1-equivalent and miss B1 network topology — is accurate. I have reviewed PROTECT program documentation and the evaluation criteria (FEMA SFHA miles, historical event frequency, projected hazard intensity) do not include any measure of alternate route availability. A corridor applying for PROTECT grants could have perfectly adequate alternates (B1 = 2.0) and receive the same evaluation score as Donner Pass (B1 = 8.3), because the criteria don't ask the question.

The recommendation to add compound score weighting at "no less than 30% of total application score" is specific and actionable. The paper correctly notes this does not require statutory amendment — FHWA has authority to define grant evaluation criteria under existing IIJA Title 23 authority. This is the right level of specificity for a policy recommendation.

The phased investment program (Section 5.3) reflects a realistic understanding of federal appropriations constraints. The identification of the Donner tunnel as requiring dedicated authorization (not just PROTECT formula funding) is important and honest — too many academic papers recommend investments that have no plausible funding path.

The I2.0 complementarity argument (Section 6.5) is well-made. The point that managed freight lanes and compound-exposure hardening address different failure modes and are not substitutes is important for practitioners who might see them as competing budget items.

## What Doesn't Work

**The PROTECT reform recommendation needs an implementation pathway.** Stating that FHWA "has authority to define grant evaluation criteria under existing IIJA Title 23 authority" is true but insufficient. The PROTECT program has an established Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) process, with evaluation criteria defined through a public comment period. Changing those criteria requires: (a) FHWA publishing a revised NOFO with updated criteria; (b) a comment period (typically 60 days); (c) FHWA response to comments. This is a 9-12 month process for the next PROTECT discretionary grant round. The paper should acknowledge this timeline and recommend that the ROUTE compound score methodology be submitted for inclusion in the next PROTECT NOFO development cycle (anticipated FY2027). Without this specificity, the recommendation remains academic.

**The National Critical Highway Infrastructure designation pathway is not clearly established.** The paper cites 49 U.S.C. § 70101 et seq. for the National Critical Highway Infrastructure designation and lists four authorities triggered by this designation. I am familiar with the Transportation Security Administration's critical infrastructure authorities, but I am not aware that 49 U.S.C. § 70101 provides the specific designation powers described in the paper — specifically the "mandatory federal funding participation above normal NHS apportionment formula." The citation should be verified against current statutory text, because if the authority doesn't exist in the form described, the entire Section 6.3 recommendation is speculative. A reviewer with statutory expertise should check this before publication.

**The DoD military logistics argument for Donner and Snoqualmie is underdeveloped.** Section 6.3 mentions that Donner and Snoqualmie "serve Pacific military logistics" as justification for Defense Infrastructure Protection fund access. This is true but requires more specificity to be a policy-grade argument. What specific defense installations and supply chains use I-80 Sierra crossing? What is the military freight volume (classified or estimated)? The DoD has its own infrastructure criticality assessments under the Installations Resilience program — does Donner Pass appear in those assessments? Without this, the defense funding argument reads as opportunistic rather than analytically grounded.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper recommends PROTECT grant reform to include compound score weighting. But PROTECT is a discretionary program — even with better evaluation criteria, there is no guarantee that compound-exposure corridors receive funding, because PROTECT is competitive and states control which projects they submit. California could theoretically submit a Donner Pass resilience project for PROTECT funding; it has not done so in the three PROTECT cycles to date. Is the reason that PROTECT criteria don't reward compound exposure, or that the required federal share and project complexity make PROTECT an unattractive vehicle for a project of this scale? If it's the latter, the PROTECT criteria reform doesn't solve the problem — what's needed is a dedicated large-project resilience authority, analogous to the BUILD or INFRA programs but with a minimum project size trigger ($500M+) and automatic federal share for nationally critical corridors. Has the paper considered this alternative policy pathway?
