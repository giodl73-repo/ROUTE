## Headline Assessment

The paper makes a genuine contribution in three areas: (1) PTI 1.15 as the operational reliability threshold enabling timetable scheduling; (2) identification of 5 corridors with no current service where T1 bus addresses genuine market and equity gaps; (3) in-between community equity analysis naming specific communities with high transit-dependency rates. Two structural issues block promotion to reviewed status: (1) the 62 mph average speed figure is not supported by a stop penalty model, and all downstream travel-time and competitiveness findings depend on it; (2) the 24M passenger gravity model is not described with sufficient methodological transparency. The EIT designation is a P1 policy issue (carried from F.1). The managed lane access allocation mechanism is a P2 design gap that should be resolved before the paper advances to validated status.

---

## Earned Stakes (E1–E5)

**E1 — PTI 1.15 threshold earns full consensus.** All five reviewers accept that managed lane reliability (PTI 1.15) is the necessary precondition for viable timetabled intercity bus service. McKinnon validates it from the freight lane design perspective; Walker validates it from the service scheduling perspective; Puentes accepts it as the policy-relevant infrastructure metric. This finding is the paper's most durable contribution and should be foregrounded.

**E2 — Five no-current-service corridors earn consensus as genuine market gaps.** No reviewer disputes that Atlanta-Dallas, Houston-Chicago, Chicago-Minneapolis on managed lanes, Denver-SLC, and the key Gulf Coast corridor represent genuine unmet demand. Walker and Neumark both identify these corridors as the strongest demand cases in the paper — precisely the corridors where gravity model calibration on existing bus routes most severely understates potential demand.

**E3 — In-between community equity finding earns qualified consensus.** Schmitt, Puentes, and Neumark all accept the named-community approach (Coalinga, Effingham, Durant) as the right methodology. Schmitt adds the road safety dimension; this is a strengthening addition, not a challenge to the finding.

**E4 — The 62 mph speed claim does not survive scrutiny.** Walker and McKinnon independently identify the stop penalty problem. Walker's analysis is quantitative: at 8 intermediate stops with 5-minute penalty each, the effective average speed on a 500-mile corridor is 54-55 mph, not 62 mph. This is not a rounding error — it changes the travel-time competitiveness finding materially on several corridors. This is a P1 blocking item.

**E5 — The 24M demand estimate requires methodological transparency.** Neumark and Walker both independently flag the calibration gap. Neumark provides the upper-bound reasoning: T1 bus would represent a 60-80% increase in total US intercity bus ridership, which is possible but requires explicit modeling of mode-shift from auto and air, not just extrapolation from existing bus ridership. This is a P1 blocking item.

## P1 Blocking Items

**P1.1 — Add a stop penalty model for the 62 mph average speed claim.** For each of the 12 corridors, specify: (a) number of intermediate T1/T2 stops in the timetabled schedule; (b) dwell time per stop; (c) acceleration/deceleration penalty per stop at managed lane speeds; (d) resulting stop-penalty-adjusted average speed. If the adjusted speed on multi-stop corridors is below 62 mph, revise the travel-time competitiveness findings accordingly. Distinguish limited-stop express service (62 mph achievable) from all-stops service (lower average). *(Walker, McKinnon)*

**P1.2 — Provide gravity model methodological detail sufficient for replication.** Add a methodology section or appendix specifying: calibration corridor set (routes, years, ridership data sources); impedance function form; mode-specific constants and how they are adjusted for T1 bus product attributes; model R² against calibration data; mode-shift assumption (what percentage of 24M passengers are attracted from existing bus vs. auto vs. air). *(Neumark, Walker)*

**P1.3 — Address EIT designation as legislative recommendation, not existing authority.** Follow F.1 P1.3 resolution: either characterize the statutory pathway or replace with existing FTA 5311(f) authority. Derive the $15/passenger subsidy cap from corridor cost models. *(Puentes)*

## P2 Important Items

**P2.1 — Propose a managed lane access allocation mechanism.** Address: time-of-day access windows (bus priority vs. freight priority), speed compatibility between bus and freight operations, and the regulatory or contractual framework for access rights. This can be a design proposal, not a finalized policy — but the gap must be named and at least one allocation model must be proposed. *(McKinnon, Puentes)*

**P2.2 — Add road safety dimension to equity analysis.** Estimate the safety benefit of shifting in-between community travel from rural state routes (high fatality rates) to managed freight lanes (limited access, lower fatality rates per VMT). Use FHWA value-of-statistical-life methodology ($11.6M, 2023 guidance). *(Schmitt)*

**P2.3 — Add price elasticity scenario to demand model.** Provide a simple low/base/high demand scenario (e.g., ±30%) showing how passenger numbers and operator revenue change with fare adjustments. *(Neumark)*

**P2.4 — Add decarbonization benefit estimate.** Calculate the mode-shift CO2 reduction from 24M passengers shifted from auto and air modes. Use EPA emissions factors for car travel and commercial air travel vs. intercity bus. *(McKinnon)*

**P2.5 — Add Amtrak vs. T1 bus comparison table.** Present travel time, fare, and frequency comparison for all 12 corridors in tabular form. This is the paper's strongest competitive positioning finding and it deserves a dedicated exhibit. *(Puentes)*

## Next Steps

1. P1.1 (stop penalty model) and P1.2 (gravity model methodology) are the most analytically demanding revisions. Both require working through the quantitative model in detail.
2. P1.3 (EIT) follows the same resolution path as F.1 P1.3 and can be addressed consistently across both papers.
3. Walker and Neumark re-review of P1.1 and P1.2 respectively are the critical gates for round 2.
4. P2.1 (managed lane allocation) is the most policy-complex item and may benefit from engagement with freight operator stakeholders or reference to existing managed lane HOT/HOV allocation models (e.g., I-405 Express Toll Lanes in Washington state).
