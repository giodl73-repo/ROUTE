## Overall
The paper's freight economics are strong and the relay driver labor market analysis is excellent. My concerns are primarily about scope discipline: C.3 is a paper about freight economics, but it repeatedly gestures toward transit access and passenger mobility in ways that belong in F.1 and F.2. The conflation weakens both arguments — the freight case is diluted by transit language, and the transit case gets hand-waved rather than analyzed. Separate them cleanly and both become stronger papers.

## What Works

**The relay model is correctly analyzed as a freight innovation, not a transit innovation.** The core mechanism — six drivers replacing one, each working a seven-hour regional leg and sleeping at home — is a freight logistics innovation that happens to have transit-like labor economics. The paper is right to emphasize the UPS Worldport and BNSF crew-change analogies over any transit analogy; these are the structurally correct comparisons.

**The SLA analysis is exactly what freight customers need.** The p50/p75/p95 distribution and the 98.8% vs 88% SLA comparison are the right way to communicate reliability to freight shippers. Transit planners obsess over frequency and reliability in exactly this way; it is good to see the same rigor applied to freight. The simulation results from C.1 are used correctly.

**The economic decomposition by commodity class is well-executed.** Table 1's breakdown of coast-to-coast air cargo by commodity type, with addressability percentages for each, is the kind of structured analysis that enables readers to disagree with specific assumptions rather than the whole estimate. This is good scientific practice.

**The "Layer 0" concept is the paper's strongest strategic insight.** The argument that relay stations are foundational infrastructure that can precede managed lanes and still deliver partial economic returns is an important policy finding. This maps cleanly to how transit planners think about phased infrastructure deployment — you can run better bus service before you build BRT before you build rail, and each phase delivers value independently.

## What Doesn't Work

**The relay hub as transit node creates conflation.** Section 7.3's Freight Network Operator framework and Section 6's relay station community anchor discussion both gesture toward the relay hub's role as a transit node — connecting relay drivers to the station, enabling intercity passenger service, creating multimodal hubs. These are legitimate topics, but they belong in F.1 and F.2. Every time C.3 mentions passenger transit, it opens a cost and operations analysis that the paper is not equipped to close. The relay station's transit function has a different cost structure, different regulatory framework, and different political economy than its freight function. Keep them separate.

**The "overnight sleeper in your own car" claim needs full separation from freight SLA claims.** Section 5 mentions that autonomous or semi-autonomous freight enables "overnight freight movement while the driver sleeps" as a future extension. This is speculative and logistically distinct from the relay model the paper actually analyzes. It should be cut or moved to a future-work paragraph in the conclusion; as written it blurs the boundary between the paper's actual findings (relay driver model) and a hypothetical (autonomous freight) that could undermine the relay driver job creation argument.

**The relay hub's capital cost at $5M per station is not decomposed.** The paper presents $5M per station as a given (sourced from C.1) but does not show what is included: land, construction, equipment, technology. For a JEP paper whose core policy claim is that $40M unlocks $8.2B/year in benefits, the $40M figure needs at minimum a line-item explanation so readers can evaluate whether it is plausible. As Puentes will note, this figure is almost certainly for construction only, not total development cost.

## The Question I'd Push On
The relay hub is described in both this paper (as a freight handoff facility) and in F.1/F.2 (as an intercity transit node). What is the incremental capital cost of making a freight relay hub a functional intercity transit node — and does that incremental cost change the benefit-cost analysis for either the freight case (C.3) or the transit case (F.1)? Answering this question clearly would also eliminate the scope conflation issue.
