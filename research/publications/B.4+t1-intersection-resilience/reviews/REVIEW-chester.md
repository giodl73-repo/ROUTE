## Overall

The paper proposes a substantial capital investment at 15 T1/T1 intersections with a 30–50-year asset life, yet its resilience analysis is framed exclusively in terms of graph-theoretic k-connectivity. This is a useful but incomplete resilience framework — it captures topological failure modes but misses the physical hazard exposures that will increasingly drive realized closure events over the asset lifetime. For a paper titled "intersection resilience," the absence of climate hazard analysis is a significant gap. That said, the core methodology is sound, the NPV analysis is competent, and the finding on the spatial distribution of k=1 intersections is valuable. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The paper's core argument — that k=1 intersections are disproportionately vulnerable in the national freight graph — is well-supported. The B2_product prioritization correctly focuses investment where junction failure causes cascading impact across both intersecting corridors, not just one. The diamond interchange zone concept is a genuine structural improvement: by distributing the interchange function over 50 miles with parallel access paths, the proposal converts a topological singularity to a redundant zone.

The three priority sites are geographically diverse — Southeast (Atlanta), Southeast coastal (Jacksonville), and Midwest (Toledo) — which is appropriate for a national program. The cost and NPV estimates, while requiring more supporting detail, are in a range consistent with comparable FHWA programs. The B/C ratio of 2.76:1 compares favorably to published estimates for similar interchange investments.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper does not analyze the physical hazard exposure of the 15 T1/T1 intersection sites. Jacksonville (I-10/I-95) sits in a FEMA Zone A floodplain with significant sea-level rise exposure through 2050–2070, the design life of the proposed diamond zone. Toledo (I-75/I-90) sits in the Great Lakes basin; the intersection is proximate to Maumee River flood zones. Atlanta's I-75/I-85 merge corridor traverses urban heat island conditions that affect pavement performance and incident frequency.

A k=1 classification today is a necessary but not sufficient basis for prioritizing a diamond zone investment. An intersection that is k=1 but faces low hazard exposure over its design life is a different investment case than one that is k=1 and sits in a 100-year flood zone projected to experience 500-year flood recurrence by 2060. The paper should at minimum cross-reference intersection locations with FEMA FIRM maps and NOAA sea-level-rise projections, and adjust the NPV analysis to reflect expected physical disruption costs under baseline and stressed climate scenarios.

A secondary concern: the access-controlled freight lane within the diamond zone is a new infrastructure element with its own climate exposure. If the parallel freight lane is routed at grade through a floodplain to provide the k-connectivity alternative, it provides no resilience benefit during a flood event — the failure mode shifts from topological to physical, and the investment does not address it.

## The Question I'd Push On

For each of the 15 intersections, what is the projected physical disruption probability over the 30-year NPV horizon — from flood, extreme heat (pavement buckling, bearing failure), or severe storm events — and how does that probability affect the expected NPV? The $12.4B portfolio NPV is computed under a deterministic traffic growth assumption. If Jacksonville's diamond zone is inundated one week per decade under mid-range SLR scenarios, the NPV deteriorates materially. Physical resilience analysis should be integrated into the investment case, not treated as a separate research question.
