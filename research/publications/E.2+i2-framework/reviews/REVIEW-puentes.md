## Overall

The paper makes a technically coherent case for Interstate 2.0 as a targeted upgrade program rather than a new-build system, and the integration of six investment components under a single NPV framework is a genuine contribution. But the paper's fatal weakness is treating $253B over 30 years as a straightforwardly achievable funding reality when it is not. The federal highway funding landscape is not an inert substrate that the paper can assume into existence. This is a $8.4B/yr sustained federal commitment — roughly 40-50% of the current IIJA annual highway apportionment — dedicated to a specific corridor list. That would require either a complete restructuring of the Surface Transportation Block Grant formula or new dedicated authorization. The paper does not address this once. Until that political-economy gap is filled, the paper's policy conclusions float free of the institutional terrain they would have to navigate.

## What Works

The synthesis architecture is the paper's real strength. Pulling six investment components — managed freight lanes, diamond interchanges, compound exposure hardening, missing links, intermodal integration, EV charging — under a single B/C framework (2.2:1 overall) is exactly what a synthesis paper should do. The phasing structure (Phase 1 $89B / Phase 2 $96B / Phase 3 $68B) provides a credible front-loading logic: the highest-NPV components (managed lanes and missing links) come first. The PTI variance reduction finding (std dev 0.42→0.18) is compelling and well-grounded in the B.2 and C.1 track findings.

The 0.8% transit layer is a genuinely elegant framing. Demonstrating that $2B on a $253B program unlocks 9 T1/T1 hubs reframes the transit conversation — this is not a transit investment, it is a co-location efficiency on a freight investment. That reframing is politically durable and the paper should lead with it harder.

## What Doesn't Work

The political economy of funding is entirely absent. The paper states that $8.4B/yr is "comparable to current IIJA annual highway apportionment" as if comparability implies feasibility. It does not. IIJA apportionment flows through formula-driven state DOT allocations. Converting that into a dedicated corridor-specific federal priority list requires either a new earmark structure (politically toxic post-2005 ethics reform) or a merit-based competitive program with no precedent at this scale. The paper should at minimum acknowledge the Surface Transportation Reauthorization cycle (next due ~2026-2027) and identify which funding titles I2.0 would require.

The aggregate $298B NPV figure is not sufficiently decomposed. The six component NPVs are reported, but the aggregation arithmetic is not shown. If managed lanes ($121B capital) produce the bulk of the NPV, the aggregate figure is sensitive to that component's benefit assumptions. A sensitivity table showing NPV at 5%, 7%, and 9% discount rates — and with ±25% benefit uncertainty — would materially strengthen the result.

The corridor selection methodology for missing links ($88B: I-69/I-31/I-29S/I-92/I-70W) is referenced but not integrated into the synthesis. A reader should be able to see why these five corridors and not others. The connection to the ROUTE rubric gap map is asserted but not demonstrated in the paper itself.

## The Question I'd Push On

If the IIJA reauthorization in 2026-2027 does not create a dedicated I2.0 program title, what is the implementation pathway? Specifically: can Phase 1 ($89B over 2025-2035) be assembled from existing INFRA grants, BUILD grants, and state DOT allocations without new authorization — and if so, what percentage of Phase 1 is realistically achievable through existing funding vehicles? The paper needs either an honest answer or an honest acknowledgment that new legislation is a precondition.
