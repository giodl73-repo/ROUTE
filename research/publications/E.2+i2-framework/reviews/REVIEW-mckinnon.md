## Overall

A technically accomplished synthesis that assembles a large capital program into a coherent benefit-cost architecture. The freight benefit case — throughput increase from 4.8M to 7.2M commercial vehicles/day, PTI variance halved — is substantive and the managed lanes component is correctly identified as the highest-value single intervention. My central concern is that the paper treats decarbonization as peripheral when it is structurally central to the long-term viability of the investment case. A 30-year infrastructure commitment that does not address mode-neutral freight decarbonization pathways is not future-proofed. The EV charging component ($8B) is the smallest line item and receives the least analytical attention; this is exactly backwards given the policy environment that will govern the 2035-2055 Phase 3 period.

## What Works

The managed freight lane case ($121B capital, largest single component) is well-argued. The throughput increase from 4.8M to 7.2M commercial vehicles/day (50%) is plausible given the degree to which truck productivity is currently constrained by mixed traffic in bottleneck corridors. The connection to C.1 (O-D freight reliability) and B.2 (freight bottlenecks) track findings is properly integrated. The PTI variance reduction (0.42→0.18) is the most policy-actionable single metric in the paper — shippers price schedule reliability and this finding should be translated into shipper-side benefit estimates (reduced inventory carrying cost, reduced safety stock).

The diamond interchange component ($4.5B) is the paper's most efficient investment per dollar — the B/C ratio here is almost certainly the highest of the six components, and surfacing that explicitly would strengthen the aggregate case.

The three-phase structure correctly sequences the highest-NPV components first, which is appropriate for a 30-year program that will face political pressure at each reauthorization cycle.

## What Doesn't Work

Decarbonization integration is inadequate. The freight sector accounts for approximately 28% of US transportation GHG emissions; a 50% increase in commercial vehicle throughput on managed lanes raises the question of absolute emission trajectory directly. The paper should model two scenarios: (1) managed lane expansion with current fleet mix (diesel-dominant), producing absolute emission increases even with efficiency gains; (2) managed lane expansion with electrification trajectory (EV charging + zero-emission vehicle mandates by 2035-2040). The $8B EV charging figure is reported without a coverage analysis — how many miles of managed freight lane per charging point, what is the range requirement for Class 8 BEV trucks at 2030 vs. 2040 technology assumptions?

The intermodal integration component ($12B) is underanalyzed relative to its strategic importance. Intermodal integration is where managed freight lanes and rail freight can complement rather than compete. The paper should identify which of the five missing link corridors have parallel rail rights-of-way and whether intermodal facilities could be co-sited with I2.0 managed lane terminals.

The aggregate NPV ($298B at 7%) would benefit from a mode-shift sensitivity: if freight demand growth is partially captured by rail electrification (driven by federal rail investment or carbon pricing) rather than road freight, what is the impact on the managed lane throughput assumption?

## The Question I'd Push On

The 2035-2055 Phase 3 period ($68B) coincides with the period when Class 8 zero-emission vehicle technology is most uncertain. If battery-electric trucks achieve 500-mile range by 2040 (plausible on current trajectory), the optimal charging infrastructure layout changes significantly from a 2025 design. What is the paper's update mechanism — how does the I2.0 framework accommodate EV infrastructure redesign mid-program without stranded capital in Phase 2 charging investments?
