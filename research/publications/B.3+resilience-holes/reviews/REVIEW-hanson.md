## Overall

The spatial analysis underlying the compound resilience hole concept is methodologically sound and the geographic distribution of the 11 identified corridors is credible. The paper correctly identifies that compound exposure is not uniformly distributed across the national network — it concentrates in specific geographic contexts: mountain passes (Donner, Snoqualmie, Siskiyou, Homestake), coastal lowlands (Gulf Coast I-10), and severe weather corridors (I-35 Oklahoma). This geographic specificity is the paper's most important empirical finding, and the paper should foreground it more strongly. My concerns are about what the spatial analysis misses: the geographic communities that depend on these corridors and bear disproportionate disruption cost when they close, and the spatial equity implications of the proposed investment sequencing.

## What Works

The spatial distribution of compound exposure corridors is well-characterized. The distinction between mountain-pass compound exposure (high B1 from terrain barriers, high D1 from winter precipitation) and coastal compound exposure (moderate B1 from industrial port concentration, high D1 from sea level and hurricane exposure) is geographically meaningful and produces different investment implications — as the paper correctly shows in the contrast between the Donner tunnel (eliminates exposure) and Gulf Coast hardening (reduces inundation depth).

The B1×D1 product metric produces a geographic priority ordering that is intuitive from a spatial perspective: the Sierra Nevada crossing (Donner) ranks first because there is no geographic alternative — the Sierra Nevada is a hard barrier, not just a routing inconvenience. The Cascades (Snoqualmie) and Klamath Mountains (Siskiyou) rank lower because they are less formidable barriers and lower-freight corridors. The Gulf Coast ranks high because sea-level-rise trajectory is the steepest in the portfolio. This geographic logic is coherent and the paper should make it more explicit.

The 2050 trajectory discussion (Section 6.4) is geographically important. The observation that Gulf Coast I-10's compound priority ranking will approach Donner's by 2040 under mid-range SLR scenarios reflects a genuine geographic shift: the Sierra Nevada is a stable barrier (B1 will not change), while the Gulf Coast is a dynamic barrier (D1 will increase). The investment implications of this trajectory — begin Gulf Coast design concurrently with Donner authorization — are geographically correct.

## What Doesn't Work

**The geographic communities dependent on compound-exposure corridors are absent.** The paper analyzes freight disruption costs (tons, truck-hours, dollars) but does not characterize the human communities that depend on these corridors. Donner Pass I-80 serves not only transcontinental freight but also the communities of Truckee, Kings Beach, Tahoe City, and South Lake Tahoe — tourist and resort communities whose economic activity is disproportionately affected by closure events. When I-80 closes at Donner, these communities are isolated: food supply chains, healthcare access, and emergency services are interrupted for the same 18-hour mean closure duration that strands 8,000 trucks. The paper's freight-centric analysis misses this community dependency, which is particularly acute for the mountain pass and Cascade corridor cases.

**The spatial equity of the investment sequencing is not analyzed.** The phased investment program prioritizes Donner (Phase 1), Gulf Coast Louisiana (Phase 2), Snoqualmie (Phase 3). This sequencing benefits California, Louisiana, and Washington — states with large congressional delegations and existing resilience program experience — before reaching Montana (Homestake Pass, Phase 4) or Oklahoma (I-35, Phase 4). The geographic distribution of benefits is not spatially equitable by any measure: the last-phase corridors serve lower-income, more rural states with less federal lobbying capacity. The paper should acknowledge this and note whether the compound score priority order can be defended on equity grounds (it serves the highest national-freight-impact corridors first) or whether a spatial equity adjustment is warranted.

**The B1 score's geographic sensitivity to altitude and terrain is not discussed.** The paper notes that B1 = 10 corresponds to corridors with no interstate-standard alternate, and gives mountain passes as the primary cases. But B1 scores for mountain passes are sensitive to the specific geometric constraints of the terrain — a pass at 7,200 feet (Donner) with no viable alternate within 310 miles has a different B1 profile than a pass at 3,000 feet (Snoqualmie) with alternates at 60 additional miles. The paper should discuss how the geographic character of the barrier (altitude, relief, pass geology) translates to the B1 score, so readers can understand why Donner's B1 = 8.3 while Snoqualmie's is 7.6 despite similar functional roles as Cascade/Sierra crossings.

**The spatial pattern of compound exposure concentration is not generalized.** The paper identifies 11 corridors but does not ask: what geographic conditions produce compound exposure? The answer appears to be: (1) mountain passes on major transcontinental freight corridors with no parallel geographic crossing; (2) coastal lowlands serving major ports with high sea-level exposure. These are two distinct geographic types that produce compound exposure through different mechanisms. The paper should make this geographic typology explicit — it would help practitioners identify other potential compound exposure corridors not in the current corpus.

## The Question I'd Push On

The compound resilience hole concept applies to highway corridors, but the geographic barrier that produces high B1 — a mountain range or coastal wetland — often also affects rail and air capacity in the same corridor. When I-80 closes at Donner, Union Pacific's transcontinental mainline (which also crosses the Sierra Nevada at approximately the same location) faces different but related constraints. When Gulf Coast I-10 floods, rail and highway in the same corridor are simultaneously at risk. Has the paper considered the geographic co-location of highway and rail compound exposure, and whether a corridor-level investment in hardening (tunnel, roadway elevation) could be structured as a joint highway-rail investment that addresses compound exposure across modes simultaneously? The geographic alignment of highway and rail in these corridors makes co-investment analysis straightforward, and a joint NPV calculation might produce a substantially stronger public investment case than the highway-only analysis.
