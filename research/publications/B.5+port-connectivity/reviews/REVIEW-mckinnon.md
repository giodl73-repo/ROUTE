## Overall

A competent and well-evidenced paper on a topic that has been underserved in the port connectivity literature. The investment case is structured correctly, the V/C analysis is credible at the corridor level, and the Laredo case study adds a genuinely novel dimension — the land border connector as a distinct sub-category of the port connector problem. My main concerns are: (1) the Laredo delay cost figure bundles direct and indirect costs in a way that will not survive peer review at an economics-oriented journal; (2) the construction cost range is too wide to be analytically useful; and (3) the paper misses the opportunity to integrate the drayage electrification transition, which changes the calculus for connector design significantly. These are manageable revisions, not structural failures.

## What Works

The port connector investment case is the paper's genuine contribution. Using port TEU volume as the demand base for NPV calculation, rather than standard AADT, is methodologically correct for this application — drayage demand is port-driven, not population-driven, and the paper is right to treat it that way. The $1.86B/year delay cost for I-710 is a large number but is in the right order of magnitude given what SCAG and LA Metro have published on the I-710 corridor.

The comparative framework — ranking connectors by failure mode rather than simply by V/C — is useful and gives the paper analytical structure that goes beyond a ranking exercise. The four failure modes (capacity, classification, resilience, jurisdictional) correctly identify that different connectors require different interventions: you can't solve a jurisdictional fragmentation problem with a capacity addition.

The investment cost benchmarking against INFRA grant awards is a reasonable approach. The INFRA program has funded a number of port connector improvements in the 2019–2024 cycle, and using these as cost comparables is defensible.

## What Doesn't Work

**Laredo $10.8B: direct vs. indirect cost decomposition.** The $10.8B/year figure is described as "border delay cost," but its components are not disaggregated. At $225/hr truck operating cost × 3hr average wait × 16,000 trucks/day × 365 days, the direct delay cost computes to approximately $3.9B/year — not $10.8B. The $10.8B figure presumably incorporates JIT disruption cost: the additional supply chain cost imposed on manufacturing firms that cannot buffer inventory against unpredictable crossing delays. JIT disruption cost is real and well-documented in the border economics literature (Hummels 2001; Carballo et al. 2016), but it is an indirect cost that is model-dependent and hard to validate. The paper should present: (a) direct delay cost (the $3.9B calculation, auditable from first principles); (b) JIT disruption cost (the additional $6.9B, with an explicit citation to the econometric model used); and (c) total (the $10.8B). Bundling them without decomposition will attract referee skepticism at any economics journal.

**Construction cost range ($2–5B/connector) is too wide.** A 2.5× range means the paper cannot distinguish between high-return and marginal-return investments in the portfolio. The variance is real — urban land acquisition in Los Angeles is categorically different from Savannah — but the paper should present the cost model transparently: land acquisition (% of total, by urban density tier), structure cost (per lane-mile), utility relocation, and environmental mitigation. This would allow readers to apply the model to other ports and would substantially strengthen the investment prioritization framework.

**Drayage electrification transition.** The paper treats drayage as a diesel-intensive, high-pollution activity — which it is today. But the policy trajectory is clear: California's Advanced Clean Trucks regulation mandates 100% zero-emission new drayage trucks at CARB-regulated ports (POLA/POLB) by 2035. BYD and Daimler Freightliner's eCascadia are already operating at LA/Long Beach. This transition matters for port connector design in two ways: (a) electrification reduces the air quality argument for connector improvements (currently, I-710 is a NOx/PM2.5 hotspot that supports the environmental justice case for investment), which partially removes a policy rationale; (b) electric drayage trucks have shorter range and different operating patterns than diesel (typically 150-mile range, depot-charged), which may alter peak demand patterns on connectors as trucks return to charging depots between shifts rather than queuing for multiple gate turns. The paper should acknowledge this transition and address whether the port connector standard it proposes needs to account for EV charging infrastructure at connector facilities.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper argues that port connector investment produces "a higher throughput-per-dollar return than equivalent T1 managed-lane expansion." This is a central claim and I believe it is likely true, but the paper does not make the comparison directly — it states the connector NPV and separately states T1 NPV figures from prior literature without putting them on a comparable per-ton-mile-per-dollar basis. Can the authors produce a single table comparing: (a) port connector investment cost per TEU of throughput unlocked; (b) T1 managed lane investment cost per equivalent ton-mile of throughput unlocked; using a consistent USDOT BCR framework? If this table holds up, it is the paper's strongest result. If it doesn't hold up cleanly, the paper needs to hedge the claim.
