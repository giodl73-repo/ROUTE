## Overall

The paper's I-69 completion recommendation is the clearest policy finding and the most actionable. However, the implementation analysis understates the complexity of the I-69 completion program: approximately 400 miles of right-of-way remain unprogrammed across multiple states, several EIS processes are at different stages, and the project has been in some form of federal development since 1991. The paper should engage honestly with this history rather than presenting I-69 as a straightforward completion project. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The HOU-CHI corridor decomposition is the paper's strongest contribution. The identification of the three-hop routing (I-45→I-35→I-55) as a systemic inefficiency — adding 240 miles relative to the I-69 direct path — is a concrete, non-obvious finding with clear policy implications. The Dallas V/C 1.9+ finding, consistent with ATRI's top-10 bottleneck list, provides empirical grounding for the bottleneck claim.

The managed lane PTI target of 1.15 is appropriate and consistent with peer literature on value-pricing managed lane performance. The I-69 elimination of the Dallas and St. Louis interchange nodes is a well-motivated operational benefit that does not require the PTI model to defend.

The NY-LA section is useful as a benchmark for the national freight reliability problem, even if the PTI methodology needs revision. The Donner Pass capacity analysis — maximum throughput of approximately 91,200 vpd versus Bay Area demand — is a clean, defensible constraint that supports the investment case.

## What Doesn't Work

I-69 is not a completion project — it is an ongoing multi-generational program with deeply complicated politics. The paper presents I-69 as if it is a single project awaiting funding, when the reality is: (a) approximately 600 miles of the 1,630-mile corridor have been built to freeway standards, (b) the remaining ~400 miles requiring new construction span portions of Texas, Arkansas, Tennessee, Kentucky, Indiana, and Michigan with varying levels of state commitment, (c) multiple state EIS processes are at different stages, some stalled, and (d) federal authorizations since TEA-21 have designated I-69 as a high-priority corridor but have not provided the project-specific funding needed to complete construction.

The paper should characterize I-69 as a program, not a project, and acknowledge that the operational benefits (+18% Gulf-Chicago max-flow, -290 miles) accrue only after the full corridor is built to freeway standards — a condition that may not be met within any plausible 10-year federal investment horizon.

The paper also does not address the air freight competition point for the NY-LA corridor. A significant share of high-value freight between New York and Los Angeles moves by air — particularly time-sensitive goods that drive the high end of the shipper commitment window. The PTI reduction benefit is most valuable for time-sensitive freight; if that freight has already migrated to air, the truck PTI improvement yields primarily reliability gains for lower-value bulk and retail freight, which changes the economic case.

## The Question I'd Push On

What is the current federal authorization and funding status of the unprogrammed I-69 segments — by state, by mileage, and by estimated construction cost — and what is the realistic earliest completion date for the full corridor under current IIJA + reauthorization assumptions? A paper recommending I-69 completion as an investment priority should present this analysis. Without it, the "+18% Gulf-Chicago max-flow" finding is a theoretical result that cannot be linked to a realistic investment recommendation.
