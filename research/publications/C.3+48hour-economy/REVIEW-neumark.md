## Overall
The paper makes a compelling case that 48-hour transit is a categorical change in freight economics, and the air-substitution mechanism is real and worth quantifying. However, the core economic claims require more rigorous methodology than arithmetic applied to gross figures: the $8.2B headline is gross logistics cost savings before netting out the relay network's operating costs and before accounting for jobs displaced in long-haul trucking, and JEP readers will notice both gaps immediately. Promotion to reviewed requires addressing the net-vs-gross calculation and the labor displacement question cleanly.

## What Works

**The category-change framing is economically sound.** The paper's central claim — that 48-hour transit is qualitatively different from 87-hour transit because it crosses the threshold at which air freight becomes substitutable — is correct and well-stated. The $4/lb vs $0.35/lb cost differential is the right way to frame this. The equation in Section 3.3 is clear and the arithmetic is right on its own terms.

**The relay driver lifestyle argument is the strongest section.** Section 6's analysis of why the driver shortage is a lifestyle problem rather than a wage problem is excellent labor economics. The observation that signing bonuses address the wrong constraint is exactly right, and the comparison to airline crew bases and BNSF crew-change points is the kind of structural analogy JEP readers appreciate. This section would stand alone as a strong short paper.

**The commodity decomposition in Table 1 is useful.** Breaking the $15B coast-to-coast air market into commodity classes with distinct addressable-market fractions is the right analytical move. The exclusion of sub-24-hour urgency freight and -70°C cold chain is appropriately conservative.

**The policy sequencing insight is novel.** The observation that relay stations ($40M) can precede managed lanes ($253B) and already deliver ~88% SLA on current infrastructure is genuinely useful for policy analysis. This is the kind of sequencing insight JEP publishes.

## What Doesn't Work

**The $8.2B figure is gross, not net, and the paper doesn't say so clearly enough.** The calculation in Section 3.3 computes the difference between what shippers pay to fly cargo and what they would pay to truck it. It does not subtract: (a) the relay network's operating costs — relay station fees will be passed to carriers and ultimately to shippers, adding back some cost; (b) the lost revenue to air cargo carriers, which is a transfer, not a net social gain. The paper does note briefly that "airlines lose an equivalent revenue stream," but this is buried in a single sentence. JEP requires that the net welfare calculation be front and center, not the gross cost-transfer figure.

**The jobs analysis conflates job redistribution with job creation.** Section 6 claims 20,000 relay driver jobs as a benefit, and separately notes the driver shortage. But it never directly addresses whether relay displaces long-haul jobs on a 1:1 basis. The relay model requires the same number of truck trips (same freight volume, same number of trailers moving coast to coast). The six relay drivers who collectively replace one long-haul driver are doing the same work in distributed form. Net new jobs in trucking may be close to zero; what changes is job structure, not job count. This must be stated explicitly, or the paper is vulnerable to the accusation that it is double-counting: the relay driver jobs appear in Section 6 as a benefit, but the long-haul jobs that are restructured (not eliminated, but changed) are not discussed.

**The gravity model for e-commerce demand is underspecified.** Section 5 estimates the e-commerce fulfillment center capital savings using Amazon's 110-facility strategy as a baseline, but does not specify the elasticity of fulfillment center demand with respect to transit time. The $4–20B range is too wide to be analytically useful in a JEP paper.

## The Question I'd Push On
If the relay network's operating costs (station fees, coordination overhead) are added back into the freight cost calculation, what is the *net* freight cost saving to shippers, and how does that compare to the $8.2B gross figure? Without this number, the headline is a cost transfer, not a welfare gain.
