## Headline Assessment

The paper successfully integrates six investment components under a single benefit-cost framework and makes a credible case for $253B in targeted interstate upgrades producing $298B NPV at 7%. The synthesis architecture is sound and the PTI variance reduction metric is the paper's most analytically defensible contribution. Three structural gaps block promotion to reviewed status: (1) the $298B NPV figure is not arithmetically reconciled from component-level benefits; (2) the federal funding pathway is treated as self-evident when it requires new legislative authorization that does not currently exist; (3) community displacement and induced demand — the two central equity vulnerabilities of a highway capital program — are entirely absent. These are P1 items. Climate resilience and decarbonization integration are P2 items of secondary priority but necessary for the 30-year credibility of the program design.

---

## Earned Stakes (E1–E5)

**E1 — The synthesis architecture earns consensus.** All five reviewers accept the six-component structure, the B/C framework, and the phasing logic. No reviewer disputes that managed freight lanes are the highest-value component or that the PTI variance reduction (0.42→0.18) is a meaningful reliability outcome. This structure should be preserved in revision.

**E2 — The 0.8% transit layer framing is the paper's strongest policy contribution.** Puentes, Schmitt, and Chester all implicitly accept that co-locating intercity bus infrastructure on freight hub investments is a high-efficiency equity move. This finding — $2B unlocks 9 T1/T1 hubs — should be moved to the abstract and introduction, not buried in component six.

**E3 — The PTI variance reduction metric is the right synthesis outcome variable.** McKinnon and Chester both validate PTI standard deviation as the correct resilience metric. Schmitt notes (correctly) that it has an underexploited equity dimension: supply chain reliability disproportionately benefits freight-dependent low-income communities. The paper should exploit this connection explicitly.

**E4 — Phase sequencing logic is defensible.** Neumark, Puentes, and Chester all accept that front-loading managed lanes and beginning missing link construction in highest-demand corridors is the correct sequencing. The Phase 1 → Phase 2 → Phase 3 structure provides political checkpoints that strengthen, not weaken, the program's institutional durability.

**E5 — The five missing link corridors require individual treatment.** McKinnon, Chester, and Schmitt independently note that I-69, I-31, I-29S, I-92, and I-70W serve structurally different markets with different NPV profiles, hazard exposures, and community impact footprints. Bundling them under a single $88B line item obscures important heterogeneity. The revision should disaggregate.

## P1 Blocking Items

**P1.1 — Reconcile $298B NPV from component-level benefits.** Provide a table showing: (a) benefit categories by component (freight time savings, PTI reliability value, reduced incident costs, rural connectivity value, transit access value); (b) time profile of benefit flows (front-loaded vs. back-loaded by phase); (c) aggregate undiscounted benefits and discounted NPV at 5%, 7%, and 9% discount rates. The 7% figure must be arithmetically derivable from this table. *(Neumark, Puentes)*

**P1.2 — Address the federal funding pathway explicitly.** Identify which existing federal funding titles (INFRA grants, NHPP, CMAQ, etc.) could fund Phase 1 components without new authorization, and which require new Surface Transportation Reauthorization authority. The paper need not solve the political economy problem — it must honestly characterize it. A single section ("Implementation Pathway and Legislative Requirements") is sufficient. *(Puentes)*

**P1.3 — Add displacement analysis for missing link urban segments.** Estimate right-of-way acquisition exposure for each of the five missing link corridors at the MSA level. Reference NEPA community impact assessment requirements and Community Benefits Agreement frameworks. I-69 through Houston requires explicit attention. *(Schmitt)*

**P1.4 — Disaggregate the $88B missing links line item.** Provide per-corridor capital estimates, NPV profiles, and primary benefit categories (freight throughput, rural connectivity, resilience, transit access) for each of I-69, I-31, I-29S, I-92, and I-70W. *(McKinnon, Chester, Neumark)*

## P2 Important Items

**P2.1 — Add NPV sensitivity at ±25% benefit uncertainty and ±2% discount rate.** A three-scenario table (low/base/high) for aggregate NPV is standard for infrastructure investment papers at this capital scale. *(Neumark)*

**P2.2 — Integrate decarbonization scenarios into Phase 3 analysis.** Model two freight electrification trajectories (current pace vs. accelerated mandate scenario) and show impact on managed lane throughput assumptions and EV charging adequacy. *(McKinnon)*

**P2.3 — Disaggregate hardening investment ($18B) by hazard type.** Provide at minimum a matrix: corridor × primary hazard × hardening measure category. Demonstrate that Phase 1 hardening targets the highest compound-exposure corridors identified in D.1. *(Chester)*

**P2.4 — Add PTI equity dimension.** Add one paragraph connecting PTI variance reduction to supply-chain reliability benefits for freight-dependent low-income communities. Cite literature on grocery supply chain vulnerability in low-income urban areas and agricultural commodity supply chain volatility in rural areas. *(Schmitt)*

**P2.5 — Specify $8B EV charging coverage assumptions.** State charger spacing assumptions (miles between stations on managed freight lanes), minimum coverage for Class 8 BEV range at 2030 vs. 2040 technology assumptions, and whether the $8B includes hardening for coastal high-flood-risk segments. *(McKinnon, Chester, Schmitt)*
