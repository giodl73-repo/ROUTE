\title{The Relay Marketplace: Platform Design for 48-Hour National Freight and the AV Transition}
\author{Gio Della-Libera}
\date{May 2026}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Relay driving --- handing a loaded trailer from one driver to another at an intermediate hub
so the truck runs continuously --- is cheaper than solo long-haul (\$1,050 vs.\ \$1,456 per
2,800-mile trip), doubles asset utilization (98\% vs.\ 48\% under the Hours of Service cycle),
and eliminates the rural long-haul driver shortage premium. It is not happening at scale because
no neutral inter-company relay marketplace exists. This paper diagnoses the coordination failure
blocking relay adoption and develops an integrated platform design addressing four structural
obstacles: (1) carrier fragmentation --- 70,000+ US trucking companies, 90\% operating fewer
than 10 trucks, cannot individually sustain driver pools at relay hubs; (2) a regulatory gap
in 49 CFR Part 395, which does not define a relay terminal, a relay pre-trip inspection, or
an inter-carrier ELD handoff; (3) an insurance gap for relay drivers operating outside
single-carrier employment; and (4) a truck standardization gap that makes driver handoffs
between carriers operationally expensive. We propose the Relay Display Unit (RDU) cab
standard --- modeled on Malcolm McLean's 1956 ISO shipping container --- as a five-component
interoperability interface (standardized touchscreen, RFID driver profile, ELD relay handoff
mode, OBD-validated condensed pre-trip, and load custody record). We specify a dual-mode
insurance framework: Mode~1 hub-operator W-2 employment (launchable immediately under
existing regulation) and Mode~2 independent contractor gig layer (requires 18--24-month FMCSA
rulemaking). We design a three-tier airport-gate-model hub slot system for Tier~1 alliance
carriers, Tier~2 registered carriers, and Tier~3 spot market, and show that the relay
marketplace lowers effective barriers to entry for small carriers relative to the current solo
long-haul model. Monte Carlo simulation (5,000 trips per corridor) shows relay alone reduces
NY--LA p95 transit time from 87 to 58 hours --- capturing 90\% of the improvement achievable
by \$121 billion in managed freight lane construction, at 0.02\% of the cost (\$40 million for
8 stations). We further develop the three-phase AV transition timeline showing that T1 managed
freight lanes are the purpose-built AV operating environment (no passenger vehicles, fixed
access geometry, V2I infrastructure) and that relay hubs become AV handoff nodes with
\$10.5 million in incremental investment, avoiding \$25--40 million in greenfield AV terminal
construction. Full AV deployment on T1 managed lanes yields \$3,800 per-trip savings,
recovering the \$121 billion managed lane program within 10--12 years of AV deployment at scale.
\end{abstract}
