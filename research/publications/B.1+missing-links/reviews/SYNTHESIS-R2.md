# Review Synthesis — B.1+missing-links Round 2

## Headline Assessment

The paper has materially improved since Round 1: the county centroid artifact is now quantified (48.8M corrected / 66.5M upper bound), the budget phasing is present, the construction-era equity paragraph is included, and the 1,465 node count is verified. This round of reviewers validates those improvements and identifies three new structural concerns that must be addressed before the paper reaches the recheck threshold.

The Round 2 panel confirms the paper's core contributions: the gap identification methodology is sound, the four-zone taxonomy is durable, and the C3 economic opportunity correlation is the paper's most novel empirical finding. The panel divides sharply on equity framing: Puentes and Neumark find the paper substantially ready for revision-to-recheck; Schmitt and Walker identify structural problems with the equity analysis that require significant new work before publication. Eberts finds the agricultural freight treatment inadequate but resolvable.

**Mean: 2.6/4. Minimum: 2/4. Threshold for revision advancement: 2.5/4 mean, 2/4 minimum. The paper advances to revision.**

The P1 items below must be addressed before recheck. The P2 items are important for publication quality.

## What the Panel Agrees On (Earned Stakes)

**E1 — The 30-mile standard is well-justified and accepted by all five reviewers.** No reviewer challenged the threshold. The three-rationale derivation (emergency response, agricultural freight, labor market) remains the paper's most solid methodological foundation. Walker correctly notes that the standard is vehicle-specific, not mode-neutral — but accepts it as appropriate for a highway access paper that is honest about its scope.

**E2 — The four-zone gap taxonomy is empirically grounded and durable.** All five reviewers found the Northern Tier / Appalachians / Gulf South / Rural West classification credible and policy-relevant. Eberts validates it from a rural labor market perspective; Puentes confirms it maps to existing state LRTP frameworks; Neumark recognizes the zones from regional economic research. The Type 1/2/3/4 classification within zones is also accepted.

**E3 — The C3 economic opportunity correlation is the paper's strongest empirical finding.** Four of five reviewers (Puentes, Eberts, Neumark, Walker) agree it should lead the paper — abstract, introduction, conclusion. Neumark flags the causal inference problem but agrees the correlation itself is a genuine contribution. Schmitt extends the finding but argues it must be paired with an acknowledgment of who was harmed to build the existing system.

**E4 — The phased investment structure ($71B / $60B / $161B) is a meaningful improvement over unphased presentation.** Puentes accepts this as a workable policy framing. Neumark accepts the coverage efficiency metric as appropriate for a gap analysis (not an NPV analysis). This earned stake protects the paper from the "wish list" dismissal as long as it is accompanied by honest budget contextualization.

## Priority Items

### P1 — Blocking (must address before recheck)

**P1.1 — Remove or qualify all causal language in the C3 correlation discussion.**
*(Neumark, primary; Puentes, secondary)*
The paper states the coverage gap "IS an economic opportunity gap" and implies interstate access causes economic disadvantage. The C3 correlation is descriptive. The identification problem is severe: Appalachian coal counties are economically disadvantaged for reasons predating the interstate era. The paper must: (a) reframe the C3 finding as descriptive correlation throughout; (b) cite Michaels (2008) and Chandra and Thompson (2000) as the relevant causal identification literature; (c) state explicitly that the paper does not claim to establish causality, only co-location. This is a language change throughout the paper, not a new analysis — but it is required before publication in any economics-adjacent venue.

**P1.2 — Add no-vehicle household data to the gap county analysis.**
*(Schmitt, primary; Walker, primary)*
The paper's equity framing is incomplete without acknowledging that a substantial fraction of gap-county residents cannot benefit from highway access because they do not drive. Add ACS Table B08201 (no-vehicle household share) as a column in the gap county analysis tables. Show the distribution of no-vehicle household rates across the four gap zones. If gap zones have elevated no-vehicle household rates (likely in rural Appalachia and rural Gulf South), note this as a limitation of the highway-access framing for equity purposes. This is a data addition, not a methodological revision — the data is publicly available.

**P1.3 — Add displacement risk acknowledgment for proposed corridor alignments.**
*(Schmitt, primary)*
The paper proposes 12 new or expanded corridors without any analysis of potential community displacement. At minimum, add one paragraph in the Priority Corridors section noting that: (a) all proposed corridor alignments will require environmental justice screening under EO 12898 before any NEPA process proceeds; (b) corridors passing through or near communities of color or low-income communities require early community engagement on alignment alternatives; (c) the paper's efficiency rankings are based on coverage, not alignment quality — alignment-specific equity analysis follows corridor selection, not precedes it. This does not require new analysis but prevents the paper from being read as proposing alignments that have already been screened.

**P1.4 — Distinguish vehicle-based access from mobility access throughout.**
*(Walker, primary)*
The paper uses "transportation access" and "mobility access" in several places where it means "vehicle-based highway access." These are not the same. Replace all instances of "transportation access gap" with "interstate highway access gap" and audit the equity language throughout to ensure the paper is making vehicle-access claims, not mode-neutral mobility claims. This is a precise terminological correction that strengthens rather than weakens the paper's claims.

### P2 — Important (address for publication quality)

**P2.1 — Expand agricultural freight treatment for Northern Tier and Great Plains corridors.**
*(Eberts, primary)*
The Northern Tier corridor case should include North Dakota's agricultural freight argument: ND is the #1 US producer of spring wheat, durum, sunflowers, and canola; absence of an east-west interstate forces grain trucks onto US-2, a two-lane sub-interstate-standard highway. Add a paragraph in Section 6 on the Northern Tier that quantifies the freight argument alongside the population coverage argument. Similarly expand the I-29S/US-83 (Great Plains) corridor treatment to reflect its agricultural significance.

**P2.2 — Widen cost uncertainty bands for mountain terrain corridors.**
*(Neumark, primary; Eberts, secondary)*
The $75M/mile greenfield estimate with +50%/-30% sensitivity is too narrow for I-3 (Appalachians) and Northern Tier (Montana Rockies). Add a "difficult terrain" cost scenario using $150-200M/mile for mountain segments and show the impact on corridor efficiency rankings. Historical precedents: I-40 Smokies reconstruction ~$120M/mile (2010), I-70 Vail Pass reconstruction ~$185M/mile (2011). The paper should own the cost uncertainty for its most expensive proposed corridors.

**P2.3 — Add an RUCC code verification note.**
*(Eberts, primary)*
Verify that the paper's RUCC classifications use 2020 codes (released 2023), not 2013 codes. Several Appalachian coal counties reclassified from RUCC 4-6 to RUCC 7-9 between censuses due to population loss. If the analysis uses 2013 codes, the rural/urban split in gap counties may be miscalibrated. Add a methods footnote confirming the RUCC vintage used and whether reclassifications were checked.

**P2.4 — Acknowledge the I2.0 transit integration argument but be precise about its scope.**
*(Walker, primary)*
Walker flags that the paper references I2.0 "shared transit facilities" (Section 1) without closing the loop: do these facilities address the transit-dependent gap population? Either add a brief analysis showing how I2.0 corridor transit stops would serve gap-county transit-dependent residents, or remove the transit facility reference as a feature not analyzed in this paper. Half-claims about transit integration are worse than no claim.

### P3 — Enhancements (for a stronger paper)

**P3.1 — Add a feasibility-adjusted corridor ranking.**
*(Puentes, primary)*
Add a "buildability" column to the corridor ranking table: a 1-5 scale based on existing authorization status, partial construction, state program activity, and right-of-way availability. I-69 completion (authorized 1991, active programs in TX/IN, partial construction) should be presented as the "Phase 1A" near-term achievable corridor. The efficiency metric identifies the optimal; the feasibility column identifies the achievable.

**P3.2 — Add a back-of-envelope economic development estimate for selected corridors.**
*(Neumark, primary)*
Apply the Michaels (2008) employment multiplier to the counties that would gain interstate access from the top 4 proposed corridors. Present this as a supplementary table with explicit caveats about the external validity of applying a historical multiplier to proposed future corridors. This moves the paper from "coverage efficiency" toward "economic return" — a more compelling argument for economic-policy audiences — while being honest about the uncertainty.
