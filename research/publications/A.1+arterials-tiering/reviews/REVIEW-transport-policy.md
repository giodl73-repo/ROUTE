## Overall

This paper will be useful to federal transportation planners and should be published with revision. The congestion-stress paradox finding is important and the "metro map" framing is exactly the kind of accessible presentation that policy audiences respond to. The STRAHNET comparison is well-chosen — it's the existing closest analog to what the paper is proposing. My concerns are about the policy mechanism: the paper shows the tier classification exists and is defensible, but doesn't show what federal process would implement it or how it would differ from the existing NHS/STRAHNET system.

## What Works

**The IIJA policy implication** (Section 7.2): The argument that IIJA performance metrics systematically favor stressed urban beltways over strategic arteries is important and actionable. This is the paper's highest-value policy finding. If the NHS Performance Program metrics are systematically biased toward high-AADT corridors, and if those high-AADT corridors are beltways rather than trunk lines, the current investment program is misallocated relative to strategic value.

**The "4-tier vs. binary" contrast**: The existing NHS designation is binary — you're on it or you're not. The paper's four-tier hierarchy provides more granular information for investment decisions. This is a genuine policy improvement over the status quo.

**The transportation planning document validation** (Section 5.3): 94% of LRTPs mentioning national freight corridors include the 8 T1 corridors. This is strong external validation because LRTPs are produced by state DOTs using their own methodologies — the convergence suggests the 8 corridors are being identified independently.

**Table 5 (I2.0 feature-to-tier matrix)**: The systematic mapping of I2.0 features to tier levels is the most immediately actionable output of the paper. A planner reading this can immediately see which investments belong on which tier. This is concrete policy guidance.

## What Doesn't Work

**No federal implementation mechanism.** The paper proposes a four-tier classification but does not discuss how it would be formally adopted. STRAHNET exists because DOD designates it; NHS exists because FHWA designates it; functional class exists because states report it. A tier classification that lives only in academic literature cannot drive investment decisions. What federal process would establish and update the tier designations? How would they interact with the existing NHS designation? Who has authority to move a corridor between tiers?

**The tier thresholds shifted between v1.0 and v1.1 but the paper still uses v1.0 thresholds.** The paper's Table 1 shows T1 ≥ 26.0, but after the v1.1 rubric amendment (A3 IRI cap), the thresholds are now 21/15/9. The paper's quantitative results (scores, tier counts) are therefore based on v1.0 thresholds that are no longer current. The paper should either be updated to v1.1 scores or note explicitly that it presents v1.0 results pending rubric calibration.

**Section 7.1 investment case uses order-of-magnitude costs without context.** The paper presents "$10–15M per mile" for managed freight lane construction as if this is a firm estimate. The actual range for adding lanes to a major urban interstate is $20–100M+ per mile depending on right-of-way, urban density, and environmental requirements. The cost range should be presented with appropriate uncertainty.

## The Question I'd Push On

The tier classification proposes that T1 corridors receive the full I2.0 feature set. But the paper notes I-40's V/C is already at target (0.84). Does I-40 really need managed freight lanes, or does the tier designation itself become the investment driver regardless of need? How do you prevent the tier classification from becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy where T1 designation drives investment regardless of corridor-specific evidence? The tier should inform investment decisions; it shouldn't replace corridor-specific analysis.
