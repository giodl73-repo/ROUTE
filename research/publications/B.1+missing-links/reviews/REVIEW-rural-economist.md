## Overall

The coverage analysis is technically sound and the headline finding is important. The economic opportunity correlation (Section 4.5) is the paper's most novel contribution but its most underdeveloped section. C3 score differential is a proxy for a causal claim the paper doesn't establish: that interstate access drives economic outcomes, not that poor economic outcomes and poor access happen to co-locate. The paper would be significantly stronger if it treated the economic correlation more carefully — either by making a more modest descriptive claim or by citing the identification strategies in the economics literature that do establish causality.

## What Works

**The gap is real and large.** 66.5 million people is a finding that policy audiences can act on. The fact that it comes from publicly reproducible data (Census Gazetteer + TIGER) makes it credible and verifiable. The state-level breakdown (Table 3) is useful for political economy purposes — it shows that the gap is not just in sparsely populated states but in Tennessee, Kentucky, South Carolina, and Louisiana, states with significant congressional representation.

**The Appalachian Type 4 identification** (Section 5.2): Calling out eastern Kentucky's coalfield counties as both geographically isolated and at the bottom of national economic rankings is precisely right. The ARC has documented this for 60 years. The interstate access dimension adds a specific causal mechanism — or at least a structural correlate — to that well-documented disadvantage.

**Coverage efficiency as ranking criterion** (Section 6): This is an appropriate first-pass prioritization method. It is transparent, reproducible, and avoids the common mistake of ranking infrastructure investment by raw traffic volume (which would rank I-75 Atlanta over everything). Gap population per dollar is a reasonable proxy for social welfare impact when alternatives are similar in cost structure.

## What Doesn't Work

**The economic correlation claim needs more care.** Section 4.5 states that "gap counties score 41% higher on C3" and concludes that "the coverage gap IS an economic opportunity gap." This conflates correlation with causation. The C3 dimension measures current GDP per capita, which reflects decades of economic history. Many of the gap counties in Appalachia and the rural Gulf South are economically disadvantaged for reasons that predate the interstate system (coal industry decline, agricultural consolidation, historical disinvestment) and would persist even after interstate access is provided. The paper should cite \citet{Michaels2008} and \citet{Chandra2000} for the identification strategies that do establish causal effects, and present the C3 correlation as descriptive evidence consistent with (but not proof of) the economic access hypothesis.

**The coverage efficiency metric omits freight value.** A corridor that serves 750,000 gap-county residents who are primarily agricultural producers generates different economic value than one serving 750,000 suburban commuters. The paper's corridor ranking treats all gap-county population as equivalent. For a journal focused on transportation economics (Transportation Research Part A), adding a freight value dimension to the efficiency metric — even crudely, using USDA agricultural production data by county — would substantially strengthen the economic case.

**Cost estimates need more uncertainty acknowledgment.** The $30M/mile US highway upgrade figure and $75M/mile greenfield figure are reasonable order-of-magnitude estimates for flat-terrain corridors. But I-3 through the Appalachians or the Northern Tier through Montana are not flat-terrain corridors. Historical data on mountain interstate construction (I-40 through the Smokies, I-70 through the Rockies) suggests costs 3-5× higher per mile. The sensitivity analysis (+50%/-30%) is too narrow to capture the actual uncertainty range for the mountainous corridors.

## The Question I'd Push On

The priority corridor ranking maximizes gap population served per dollar. But it does not account for induced access: when I-3 is built, the Appalachian counties it serves also gain access to the freight network, labor markets, and intermodal facilities that the interstate enables. The economic return to a corridor is not just the welfare of the currently-isolated population — it's the change in economic activity those counties will generate once connected. A back-of-envelope calculation using the Chandra (2000) employment multiplier for new interstate access would give a much stronger NPV argument for I-3 and the Northern Tier than the coverage efficiency metric alone. Why wasn't this done, and would it change the rankings?
