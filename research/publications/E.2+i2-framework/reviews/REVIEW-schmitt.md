## Overall

The paper earns credit for the transit layer framing (9 T1/T1 hubs, 0.8% of program cost) and for the equity-adjacent corridor selection methodology — the ROUTE rubric's rural connectivity dimension provides at least a structural hook for non-metro communities. But the paper does not address two of the most consequential equity issues in highway expansion history: community displacement from corridor construction and induced automobile demand. A $253B highway capital program is also one of the largest potential displacement events in US infrastructure history. The paper's silence on this is not a minor omission — it is a replication of the intellectual pattern that produced the urban interstate disasters of the 1950s-1970s. The F.1 companion paper carries more of the equity weight than this synthesis should leave there.

## What Works

The transit layer argument is the paper's strongest equity contribution. Demonstrating that $2B (0.8% of program cost) enables 9 T1/T1 intercity bus hubs reframes the equity calculus — this is not a highway program with a transit afterthought, it is a freight program that can co-serve transit-dependent populations at marginal cost. The Gary south side and San Antonio south side hub locations (referenced in F.1) suggest the corridor selection has at least partial equity awareness built in through the C3 dimension alignment.

The rural connectivity dimension in the ROUTE rubric means that the corridor selection methodology has a structural bias toward corridors serving low-density agricultural regions, which is an equity outcome even if not framed as one. I-29 South extension and I-31 (both referenced in missing links) would serve Native American communities and rural agricultural corridors that are genuinely underserved by the current interstate network.

The PTI variance reduction metric has an equity dimension the paper does not exploit: reliability improvements disproportionately benefit low-income freight-dependent communities where supply chain disruptions translate directly into food and goods access disruptions. This connection should be made explicit.

## What Doesn't Work

Displacement analysis is entirely absent. Urban interstate construction displaced an estimated 330,000-500,000 households in the 1950s-1970s (FHWA historical estimates). The missing link corridors in urban segments (I-69 through Houston and Indianapolis; any northern I-92 alignment) will require right-of-way acquisition in developed areas. The paper does not estimate displacement exposure, does not reference NEPA community impact assessment requirements, and does not discuss the Community Benefits Agreement framework that major infrastructure programs now routinely employ. This is not optional — it is the central equity vulnerability of the program.

Induced automobile demand is not addressed. A 50% increase in commercial vehicle throughput on managed freight lanes does not directly induce passenger vehicle demand, but the broader I2.0 network expansion — particularly the missing links — will induce additional automobile VMT in the corridors served. This is the "fundamental law of road congestion" (Duranton and Turner, 2011) applied at the network level. The paper should either engage with this mechanism or explicitly scope it out with a justification.

The EV charging equity dimension is missed. $8B in EV charging infrastructure on managed freight lanes primarily serves the commercial trucking industry. The geographic distribution of charging access will determine whether rural and low-income communities gain EV range capability or whether charging remains concentrated in high-traffic commercial corridors. This warrants at least a paragraph.

## The Question I'd Push On

The five missing link corridors were selected on ROUTE rubric gap analysis. What is the corridor-level displacement exposure for each? Specifically, I-69 through Houston's Third Ward, Northside, and East End communities has documented historical construction displacement concerns (Houston Tomorrow; Texas Housers advocacy record). How does the I2.0 framework ensure that these communities receive Community Benefits Agreement protection before ROW acquisition begins, rather than after?
