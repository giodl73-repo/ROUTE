## Overall

The "in-between community" finding is the paper's most important equity contribution, and it is genuine — Coalinga, Effingham, Durant, and communities like them are genuinely isolated by the current intercity transportation network. Zero-vehicle household rates of 15-28% vs. 8% national average are credible evidence of transit dependency. But the paper's equity analysis is incomplete in one dimension I find significant: the communities with the highest zero-vehicle rates and the lowest transportation access are also, statistically, the communities with the most dangerous roads. Rural highway fatality rates are 2-3x urban rates per VMT. If T1 bus service pulls these communities onto managed freight lanes, I want to know what the safety profile of T1 bus on managed freight lanes looks like compared to current bus service on mixed-traffic two-lane state routes. The equity benefit of access is real; the safety benefit of moving from rural state routes to managed freight lanes may be even larger and is not computed.

## What Works

The in-between community analysis is the right geographic focus for intercity bus equity. The paper correctly identifies that the equity gap is not between metropolitan endpoints (which have some bus service, however inadequate) but in the communities between them that have none. Zero-vehicle household rates at named communities (Coalinga CA, Effingham IL, Durant OK) with citations to ACS data is the right methodological approach — specific, verifiable, named.

The ATL-Dallas, HOU-Chicago, DEN-SLC no-current-service finding matters for equity as much as for market. These are corridors where currently the only options are private automobile or expensive air travel — there is effectively no affordable option for zero-vehicle households traveling between these metros. A T1 bus at market fares ($0.12-0.14/mile) is a genuinely new equity-relevant option on these corridors.

The $15/passenger EIT subsidy cap for underserved routes is cited (carrying over from F.1) as the equity protection mechanism for uneconomic corridor stops. If properly legislated (an issue Puentes raises), this would provide the rural in-between community access that the paper promises.

## What Doesn't Work

Road safety is the missing equity dimension. Rural communities with high zero-vehicle rates currently rely on paratransit, family rides, and infrequent bus service on dangerous rural roads. The rural fatality rate on two-lane state highways (where most current intercity bus operates in these corridors) is substantially higher than on limited-access interstates. Moving intercity bus service from rural state routes to managed freight lanes is a safety improvement that disproportionately benefits low-income riders. The paper should compute this — even as an order-of-magnitude estimate. FHWA safety value-of-statistical-life methodology ($11.6M per fatality, 2023 guidance) provides the framework.

The fare equity analysis is absent. A $0.12-0.14/mile fare on a 400-mile corridor is $48-56 per one-way trip. For a household earning $30,000/yr — the modal income in many in-between communities — this is 0.16-0.19% of annual income per trip. How does this compare to the current cost of the trip (driving cost or intercity bus where available)? The paper should show a fare-to-income ratio comparison to calibrate the equity claim against actual traveler affordability.

The paper claims that T1 bus is competitive with Amtrak on all corridors except Memphis-Chicago. But Amtrak fares on many of these corridors are heavily subsidized (and often more expensive than bus despite subsidy). The competitive benchmark should include fare comparison, not just travel time. A traveler choosing between T1 bus and Amtrak will compare price × time; price-only or time-only comparison is incomplete.

## The Question I'd Push On

Coalinga, CA has a zero-vehicle household rate cited in the paper. Coalinga is on SR-33/I-5 in the San Joaquin Valley — it is genuinely isolated by the current network. But Coalinga is also at the center of one of the highest-fatality rural road networks in California. If a T1 bus corridor on I-5/managed lanes stops at Coalinga, how do Coalinga residents reach the I-5 stop? Is there local transit on SR-33? Is the stop accessible by walking or cycling from the town center? The access question is the same as in F.1 — physical proximity to the route does not equal operational access from the community.
