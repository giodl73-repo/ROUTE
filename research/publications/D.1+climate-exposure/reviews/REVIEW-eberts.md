## Overall

The paper provides a useful policy-relevant metric for federal climate adaptation prioritization. I read it primarily for what it says about rural corridors — particularly I-80 Donner and I-90 Montana — and on that score, the paper is more promising in framing than in execution. The D1 metric is calibrated around flood-zone exposure and is straightforwardly applicable to coastal corridors; its application to winter closure corridors in the Sierra Nevada and Northern Rockies is methodologically improvised and not fully justified.

That said, I support publication after revision. ## What Works

The paper correctly identifies that Donner (I-80) and Snoqualmie (I-90) represent a distinct class of climate exposure: corridors whose operational continuity depends on maintained winter trafficability, and where climate change is shifting the risk profile in both directions (warmer winters reduce some snowpack closures but increase atmospheric river flooding). Treating these alongside Gulf Coast flood corridors in the same framework — even imperfectly — is appropriate given that they serve comparable freight volumes and have no comparable alternative routes.

The agricultural freight framing is implicit in the paper but real: I-80 Donner carries a substantial share of California-bound agricultural refrigerated truck traffic from the Central Valley to East Coast markets. A closure event of 3+ days during harvest season triggers cascade losses in perishable freight that are not captured in standard incident cost models (which assume shelf-stable commodity freight). The paper's broader point — that closure duration and route isolation interact to determine total cost — applies with particular force to perishable agricultural corridors.

The comparison of B1 (isolation penalty) scores across corridors is one of the paper's underappreciated contributions. Donner's B1=8.3 reflects what any rural freight operator knows: there is no meaningful alternate route. That isolation amplifies every other exposure metric.

## What Doesn't Work

The winter closure proxy for D1 (using Caltrans/WSDOT closure logs as a frequency count) is dimensionally incompatible with the SFHA-based scoring used for coastal corridors. The paper presents Donner D1=7.8 alongside Gulf Coast TX D1=7.8 as if these are equivalent levels of climate exposure, but the 7.8 means different things for each:

For Gulf Coast TX, D1=7.8 reflects 89 consecutive SFHA miles — a physical fact about the floodplain geography that will remain true until floodplain maps change.

For Donner, D1=7.8 reflects a historical closure frequency averaged over some unspecified time window. This is a behavioral/operational metric, not a physical geography metric. Historical closure frequency is sensitive to operational improvements (snowplowing capacity, sensor deployment, pre-treatment protocols) that have nothing to do with climate exposure. Two corridors with identical physical vulnerability could score very differently on this proxy depending on their respective DOT maintenance budgets.

The paper should either (a) use a purely physical metric for winter corridors (e.g., days per year above 32°F alternating with below-freezing, or PRISM precipitation in frozen form per unit elevation gain) or (b) clearly separate the coastal flood sub-score from the winter closure sub-score and not present a composite D1 as if these are the same dimension.

The 2050 projection for winter corridors is even more underdeveloped than for coastal corridors. The paper does not discuss how climate change is projected to affect Sierra Nevada snowpack (which is well-modeled in the literature) or the frequency/intensity of atmospheric river events (which is the dominant closure mechanism for Donner, not snowpack per se). This is a missed opportunity.

## The Question I'd Push On

Montana and Wyoming portions of I-90 serve as the primary livestock and grain corridor connecting Northern Plains agriculture to Pacific Northwest ports. These segments are also exposed to both winter closure risk and extreme heat events (which affect pavement integrity and bridge expansion joints). The paper scores I-90 Snoqualmie at D1=7.1 but does not appear to score the Montana/Wyoming I-90 segments at all.

My question: what is the D1 score for I-90 east of Snoqualmie (Montana/Wyoming segments), and why were those segments not included? If the paper's scope is limited to the top-5 nationally by D1, it should say so explicitly. If the Montana/Wyoming segments score below 5.0, that is itself a finding — it would suggest that winter corridor risk is concentrated at the mountain passes rather than the high-plains segments, which has implications for where climate-hardening investment should go.
