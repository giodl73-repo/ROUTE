## Overall

This is a policy-relevant paper with a clear number (66.5M) and a tractable priority list. The research fills a genuine gap in the policy literature: there is no published systematic coverage standard for the US interstate system, and this paper establishes one. The corridor ranking by coverage efficiency is a useful tool for federal investment prioritization. My concerns are about political feasibility framing and the omission of institutional constraints that any real implementation would face.

## What Works

**The headline number will travel.** Policy papers live or die on whether their primary finding can be communicated in one sentence. "66.5 million Americans lack adequate interstate access" is that sentence. It is large enough to be alarming, specific enough to be credible, and grounded in public data anyone can verify.

**The FHWA Future Interstate Study validation** (Section 7.2): The fact that the coverage-efficiency ranking recovers the same top-four corridors as the FHWA's volume-based study strengthens the paper considerably. It suggests the two methodologies converge on the same investment priorities despite starting from different premises. This is a reassuring consistency check that policy audiences will find compelling.

**The Type 4 category** (Economic Opportunity Gaps within gap zones): Distinguishing between geographic isolation and economic opportunity deficits is important for policy. The I2.0 framework (cited throughout) proposes different investment types for each — new corridors for Type 1/2, access spurs for Type 3, and combined corridor + development investment for Type 4. B.1 correctly identifies the Type 4 counties as the highest-priority recipients. This is the right policy prescription.

**I-14 prioritization**: Ranking I-14 (3rd) above I-73 (6th) despite the latter's higher historical political priority is a valuable reframe. The paper makes explicit that I-14's coverage efficiency exceeds I-73's, which is counterintuitive to practitioners who have seen I-73 in policy discussions for decades. This is a genuine contribution.

## What Doesn't Work

**Political economy is entirely absent.** The paper ranks 12 corridors and assigns investment costs. It does not mention that: (a) new interstate designations require a federal designation process that has produced only a handful of new corridors since 1982; (b) the Northern Tier would cross six state boundaries requiring a multi-state compact that has never been successfully negotiated; (c) I-3's Appalachian alignment would require environmental review that has blocked prior proposals for decades. The paper treats the investment decision as a clean optimization problem. Real policy implementation is messier. At minimum, a "barriers to implementation" section that acknowledges these constraints would prevent the paper from being dismissed by practitioners as naive.

**The combined corridor cost (\$292B) is not contextualized against federal highway budgets.** The IIJA (2021) allocated \$110B for roads and bridges over five years. The 12 priority corridors at \$292B represent more than 2.5× the entire IIJA road budget. The paper should contextualize this: how many years of current federal highway investment does this represent? What fraction of the gap could be closed at current investment rates? A 10-year priority subset (top 4 corridors at \$116B — roughly one IIJA authorization cycle) would be more actionable.

**The rural access spur recommendation lacks specifics.** The paper recommends "rural access spurs of ≤10 miles for Type 3 counties" at \$1-5M each but provides no example, no criteria for which communities qualify, and no estimate of how many spurs would be needed. Without this, the Type 3 recommendation reads as a deferral rather than a solution.

## The Question I'd Push On

The coverage efficiency metric ranks corridors by gap population served per dollar. But the corridors at the top of the list (I-3, Northern Tier) are also among the most politically difficult to build — multi-state, mountainous terrain, weak metropolitan constituency. The corridors near the bottom (I-57 extension, I-87 in ND) are shorter, cheaper, and more politically feasible. Has the author considered a "feasibility-adjusted" efficiency metric that weights against political and regulatory difficulty? It might suggest a different first-year investment priority — something achievable immediately rather than optimal over 30 years.
