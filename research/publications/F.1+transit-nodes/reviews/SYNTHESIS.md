## Headline Assessment

The paper's central contribution — T1/T1 hub co-location enables a $2B transit layer on a $253B freight program at 0.8% cost — is structurally sound and the cost-efficiency argument ($161/traveler vs. $2,200 standalone) is the paper's clearest quantitative finding. Two structural issues block promotion to reviewed status: (1) the 12.4M transit-dependent figure rests on a 30-mile catchment that conflates physical proximity with operational access, a category error that Walker identifies as fundamental; (2) the Essential Intercity Transportation designation is invoked as existing legal authority when it is not. These are P1 items. The spatial methodology has correctable weaknesses (uncalibrated catchment radius, rural vs. urban proxy conflation) and the rural equity analysis underprioritizes T1/T2 stops relative to T1/T1 hubs; these are P2 items.

---

## Earned Stakes (E1–E5)

**E1 — Co-location efficiency argument earns consensus.** All five reviewers accept that adding a passenger platform to a T1/T1 freight hub at $2.1M marginal cost is genuinely efficient relative to standalone terminal construction. The $161/traveler metric is accepted as the right efficiency framing if the traveler denominator is sound. No reviewer disputes the mechanism.

**E2 — C3 alignment finding (r=0.68) earns independent validation.** Hanson (spatial methods) and Schmitt (equity) both independently accept the correlation as a substantive structural finding: the freight-optimized hub location methodology produces equity-relevant outcomes as a byproduct. Hanson calls for residual analysis; this is a strengthening request, not a challenge to the finding.

**E3 — The 40+ new city pair claim is accepted as directionally correct.** No reviewer disputes the city pair connectivity expansion. Eberts and Schmitt both ask for the list of most significant rural-serving connections, which is a strengthening request.

**E4 — The 30-mile catchment is operationally indefensible for transit-dependent travelers.** Walker and Schmitt agree on this. Hanson independently requests a sensitivity analysis on the catchment radius. The consensus across three reviewers with different analytical frameworks is a blocking finding.

**E5 — The EIT designation requires legislative authorization that does not exist.** Puentes identifies this as a P1 issue. No other reviewer directly contests it, but the policy recommendations of the paper depend on it. This is a blocking policy claim.

## P1 Blocking Items

**P1.1 — Add local transit access layer to catchment analysis.** For each of the 9 T1/T1 hub locations, document: (a) what local transit services operate within 1 mile of the hub; (b) what the walking distance is from the nearest residential area served by those routes to the hub; (c) what the revised effective catchment population is when constrained to households reachable by local transit within reasonable access time (Walker criterion: 30-45 minutes). Revise the 12.4M figure accordingly or report it as an upper-bound proximity estimate with the constrained access figure as the primary metric. *(Walker, Schmitt)*

**P1.2 — Calibrate the 30-mile catchment radius empirically.** Cite existing intercity bus catchment studies or derive the appropriate radius from ACS commute-shed data for comparable hub locations. Run sensitivity analysis at 10, 20, and 30 miles; report how the traveler-served figure changes. *(Hanson, Walker)*

**P1.3 — Address the Essential Intercity Transportation designation accurately.** Either (a) frame EIT as a legislative recommendation with a characterization of the required statutory pathway (modeled on Essential Air Service but requiring new authorization), or (b) replace EIT with existing funding mechanisms (FTA 5311, CMAQ, DOT BUILD grants) that can support underserved route subsidies under current law. Derive the $15/passenger subsidy figure from corridor cost models rather than asserting it. *(Puentes)*

## P2 Important Items

**P2.1 — Extend accessibility analysis to T1/T2 stops.** Provide a condensed version of the hub-level analysis (catchment, transit-dependent population estimate, local access availability) for the ~50 T1/T2 regional stops. This is where rural access lives. *(Eberts)*

**P2.2 — Separate rural and urban zero-vehicle household populations.** Cross-reference ACS B08201 with B08301 (means of transportation to work) to identify the transit-using subset of zero-vehicle households, which is the population with genuine transit dependency rather than non-driving status. *(Hanson)*

**P2.3 — Specify hub operating cost estimate.** Add annual operating cost per T1/T1 hub (staffing, maintenance, scheduling systems, security) and identify funding source. If EIT designation is revised per P1.3, map the operating cost to the revised funding mechanism. *(Puentes, Schmitt)*

**P2.4 — Map C3 correlation residuals.** Identify hub locations where C3 is high but transit-dependent density is low (freight-priority locations with low equity relevance) and locations where transit-dependent density is high but no hub is proximate (equity gaps in the network). *(Hanson)*

**P2.5 — Add local access improvement cost estimate.** Estimate the cost of feeder bus service or shuttle service from the nearest residential transit stop to each T1/T1 hub. Even a ballpark estimate ($X M/yr per hub for minimum feeder service) would make the true cost-per-traveler-served figure more honest. *(Walker, Schmitt)*
