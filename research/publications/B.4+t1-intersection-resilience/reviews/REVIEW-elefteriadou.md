## Overall

This is a solid network resilience paper that makes a credible case for the diamond interchange zone as an engineering intervention at T1/T1 junctions. The k-connectivity framing is appropriate for the research question, and the NPV analysis is competently executed. My primary concerns are operational: the 65-mph design speed specification for the diamond zone's ramp geometry is stated without supporting design tables, the weigh station bypass claim requires more careful geometric treatment, and the paper does not address how the access-controlled zone interacts with existing interchange spacings on the intersecting corridors. These are fixable but material omissions. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The paper correctly identifies that the conventional interchange design — a grade-separated crossing with a standard diamond or SPUI — does not address k-connectivity in the graph-theoretic sense. The diamond interchange zone concept, which extends the controlled access zone to 50 miles and provides parallel freight routing options within that zone, is a meaningful upgrade over point-intersection analysis. The 65-mph design speed target is appropriate for an access-controlled freight facility; the baseline national average of 55 mph on congested corridors makes the speed target plausible.

The B2_product priority metric effectively captures the compound impact of a junction failure — if both intersecting corridors are high-betweenness, a k=1 junction is disproportionately dangerous. The top-three prioritization (Atlanta, Jacksonville, Toledo) is defensible on this criterion. The NPV range ($1.6B–$2.8B) and B/C ratio (2.76:1 portfolio average) are consistent with comparable interchange improvement studies in the FHWA literature.

## What Doesn't Work

The design speed specification for ramp geometry is stated as "65-mph design speed" without reference to AASHTO Policy on Geometric Design of Highways and Streets (the Green Book) or NCHRP Report 659. Ramp design speed on an access-controlled freight corridor is a function of the intersection angle, ramp length, and sight distance — all of which vary by site. The paper cannot specify 65-mph ramp design without at least one worked example at a representative site.

The weigh station bypass claim is potentially the most operationally significant feature of the diamond zone, but it receives only two sentences. A weigh-in-motion (WIM) system integrated into the managed lane does not constitute a weigh station bypass in the regulatory sense — most states require a physical bypass lane with enforcement infrastructure. The paper should clarify whether the proposal is: (a) WIM-only bypass for enrolled/credentialed carriers, (b) full bypass with state agreement, or (c) a recommendation for future regulatory change. The distinction matters for implementation cost and timeline.

The 50-mile zone length is stated without sensitivity analysis. For the Atlanta I-75/I-85 junction, the effective zone would overlap with the existing Perimeter (I-285) loop, creating competing access management regimes. The paper does not address how the diamond zone access standards interact with existing interchange spacings or local access points within the 50-mile corridor.

## The Question I'd Push On

For the three priority intersections, what is the assumed ramp configuration — directional, semi-directional, or loop — and how does that choice affect the stated right-of-way cost? The cost estimates ($210M–$380M) are plausible for a major interchange reconstruction but are highly sensitive to ramp type. A directional interchange at Atlanta I-75/I-85, in an already-developed urban corridor, would require significantly more ROW than a semi-directional design. The paper should show a cost breakdown that separates roadway construction, ROW acquisition, and WIM/enforcement infrastructure, with a justification for the ramp configuration assumed at each site.
