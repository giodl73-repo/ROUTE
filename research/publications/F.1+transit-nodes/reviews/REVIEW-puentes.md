## Overall

The $2B transit layer on a $253B freight program is the right policy framing — it is the paper's central contribution and it is structurally sound. My concerns are narrower than in my E.2 review: the $2B figure is plausible, the co-location mechanism is real, and the companion E.2 paper establishes the investment context. But the Essential Intercity Transportation (EIT) designation is deployed in this paper as if it were an existing federal authority, and it is not. EIT is an analogy to Essential Air Service (EAS) — a genuinely existing program under 49 U.S.C. § 41731 — but there is no equivalent statutory authority for intercity bus. The paper's policy recommendations depend on EIT designation authority that Congress has not created. This must be addressed.

## What Works

The $2B investment framing is the paper's most important policy finding. A transit layer that costs 0.8% of the total program budget but serves 9 T1/T1 hubs is a genuinely compelling efficiency argument. If the companion F.2 paper demonstrates that those hubs enable 12+ viable intercity bus corridors with 24M annual passengers, the per-dollar transit impact of the $2B is exceptional. The paper correctly presents this as a co-location efficiency, not a transit subsidy.

The hub increment cost breakdown ($2.1M per T1/T1 hub for passenger platform, parking, amenities) is the right level of specificity for a conceptual design paper. The comparison to standalone terminal construction costs ($2,200 vs. $161 per traveler-served) is clean and well-sourced if the traveler-served denominator is sound (which Walker's review questions).

The connection to the E.2 synthesis paper is properly structured — F.1 correctly positions itself as an analysis of the transit layer within the I2.0 program rather than a standalone transit investment proposal. This is the right framing and avoids the category error of evaluating a co-located facility against transit-only alternatives.

## What Doesn't Work

The Essential Intercity Transportation (EIT) designation is used as if it were an existing legal category. It is not. The paper states that underserved routes would qualify for EIT designation with a $15/passenger subsidy cap, but there is no federal statute creating this designation. The EAS analogy (Essential Air Service, 49 U.S.C. § 41731 et seq.) is instructive but not transferable without legislation: EAS was created as a condition of airline deregulation in the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978, with a specific statutory framework and dedicated funding through the USDA Rural Development fund. Creating an EIT designation for intercity bus would require comparable legislation. The paper must either (a) recommend that Congress create EIT as new legislation and characterize the legislative pathway, or (b) identify existing funding vehicles (CMAQ, FTA Section 5311 rural transit, Greyhound operating support) that could fund underserved route subsidies under current law.

The $2B figure does not include operating cost. A passenger facility with no operating support — staffing, maintenance, scheduling information systems, security — is not a functioning transit node. The paper should estimate annual operating cost per hub and identify whether the $15/passenger EIT subsidy or other funding would cover it.

## The Question I'd Push On

The $15/passenger EIT subsidy cap appears in the paper without derivation. Where does this number come from? The EAS subsidy per passenger averages approximately $200-400 for air service in rural communities (GAO-19-317). If intercity bus is analogized to EAS, the per-passenger subsidy for truly remote T1 corridors might be substantially higher than $15. What is the empirical basis for the $15/passenger cap — is it calibrated to specific corridor cost models, or is it a policy design assumption?
