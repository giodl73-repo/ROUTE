## Overall

The paper correctly diagnoses the regulatory gap as FMCSA-level and correctly identifies the three definition changes needed (relay terminal, relay pre-trip, electronic HOS transfer). The Freight Network Operator concept is novel and the statutory analogy to railroad trackage rights is the right framing. However, the paper significantly understates FMCSA rulemaking timelines — 18 months is a best-case scenario, not the base case — and the AV transition timeline in Phase 3 (full AV, 2033+) depends on regulatory infrastructure that does not exist. These are not fatal, but a paper targeting *Management Science* will need to demonstrate policy-process sophistication that this version lacks.

## What Works

The paper's most important policy contribution is the Freight Network Operator (FNO) model: a licensed neutral platform operator that manages relay matching without owning trucks or loads. The statutory analogy to railroad trackage rights (49 U.S.C. § 11321–11326) is apt and underused in the trucking context. Like trackage rights, the FNO model creates access rights to essential infrastructure (relay terminals) for carriers who do not own the infrastructure. This is the correct framing for the enabling legislation, and the paper is the first (to my knowledge) to propose it in the trucking context.

The FMCSA rulemaking targets are well-specified: relay terminal definition, relay pre-trip definition, and electronic HOS transfer. All three are achievable through notice-and-comment rulemaking without statutory change, which is the paper's most important policy observation. The distinction from the B.5+port-connectivity paper (which requires statutory change to 23 USC 103(c)) is correct and noteworthy: relay marketplace regulation is administratively achievable.

The NFRZ (National Freight Relay Zone) designation as a mechanism for federal coordination — using existing IIJA NHPP authority for multimodal freight facilities — is correctly characterized. FHWA already designates freight facilities under 23 USC 167; the NFRZ would be a sub-designation within this existing authority.

## What Doesn't Work

**FMCSA rulemaking timeline is unrealistic.** The paper states the three FMCSA definitions are achievable "in 18-24 months." FMCSA's own rulemaking history contradicts this. The FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse NPRM was published in 2014; final rule 2017 (3 years). The ELD mandate was proposed in 2014; final rule 2015 (1 year — but this was a statutory mandate under MAP-21 with congressional deadlines forcing expedited action). For a discretionary NPRM without a statutory mandate, the Reg Track database shows FMCSA NPRMs averaging 4.1 years from initiation to final rule (2010–2023 sample, GAO-24-106178). The paper should revise the timeline to 36–60 months for a realistic base case, with 18–24 months possible only if the rulemaking is included as a mandated action in the IIJA successor reauthorization (which would require specific legislative direction).

**Phase 3 AV timeline (full AV, 2033+) lacks regulatory basis.** The paper states that Phase 3 (full AV on managed lanes, 2033+) is achievable but does not address the regulatory prerequisite: FMCSA does not currently have a framework for certifying fully autonomous commercial vehicles for interstate operation. NHTSA's AV policy (AV STEP, 2022) covers testing exemptions; FMCSA's CMV AV regulatory approach is in the "research" phase as of 2024 (FMCSA-2023-0249). A framework that permits commercial AV trunking on interstate managed lanes would require a major FMCSA rulemaking (minimum 3–5 years) plus likely NHTSA coordination. The paper should acknowledge this and either narrow the Phase 3 claim or add a sentence noting the regulatory prerequisite.

**Statutory citation for FNO model.** The paper references railroad trackage rights as an analogy but does not cite the enabling statute for the trucking context. The closest existing model is the FMCSA's registered "freight broker" category (49 USC 13904), which already provides a licensing framework for entities that arrange freight without owning it. The FNO model is distinct from a freight broker (it operates physical infrastructure, not just matching), but the paper should acknowledge the existing broker framework and specify why a new FNO category is needed rather than an amended broker category. This distinction matters for the NPRM — FMCSA will ask why a new license category is required.

## The Question I'd Push On

The IIJA (2021) designated 65 national multimodal freight corridors under 23 USC 167(e). Are any of the proposed relay hub locations — the T1 diamond hub sites identified in the ROUTE analysis — already designated as National Multimodal Freight Network segments? If so, the NFRZ designation could be accomplished by a FHWA administrative action under the existing 23 USC 167 authority without new rulemaking. This would dramatically accelerate the policy pathway and would be a more publishable finding than the current FMCSA-rulemaking-dependent approach. The paper should check whether the hub locations overlap with existing NMFN designations.
