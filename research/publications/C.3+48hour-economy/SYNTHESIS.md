## Headline Assessment

The panel finds a paper with a genuine and important economic finding — the 48-hour transit threshold creates categorically different freight economics — that is not yet ready for Journal of Economic Perspectives publication. The core mechanism is correct, the simulation foundation is solid, and the relay driver labor market analysis is the paper's strongest section. Three problems block promotion: the headline economic figure ($8.2B) is gross rather than net and this is not clearly stated; the labor displacement question (what happens to long-haul driver job structure) is unanswered; and the distributional analysis of who captures the $8.2B is absent. The paper's scope also drifts into transit territory that belongs in F.1/F.2. Revision is achievable in one pass.

---

## Earned Stakes

**E1 — The 48-hour threshold is a real categorical change.** All five reviewers accept the core mechanism. The air-to-truck substitution opportunity is real: $4/lb vs $0.35/lb cost differential, a $15B coast-to-coast air cargo market, and a 60% adoption estimate produce a plausible $8.2B gross savings figure. The simulation results from C.1 (98.8% SLA at 45.4h p95) are used correctly as empirical grounding. This stake is earned without qualification.

**E2 — The relay driver model is a genuine labor market innovation.** The lifestyle-not-wages analysis of the 80,000-driver shortage is correct and well-evidenced. The comparison to airline crew bases and BNSF crew-change points is structurally apt. The health improvement argument is real and the geographic distribution of relay jobs (interior of the country, not coastal metros) is important. This stake is earned; Schmitt's equity concerns about job quality (benefits, union status) are refinements, not refutations.

**E3 — The "Layer 0" sequencing insight is novel and policy-valuable.** The finding that relay stations ($40M for NY-LA) can precede managed lanes ($253B) and deliver 88% SLA on current infrastructure is a genuine contribution to infrastructure investment sequencing analysis. All reviewers accept this finding. McKinnon notes it as the most practically important policy insight. Walker notes it maps correctly to how transit planners think about phased deployment.

**E4 — The fresh produce case is the paper's strongest commodity-specific analysis.** California's agricultural dominance (83% strawberries, 90% avocados) combined with the 48-hour shelf-life threshold is well-evidenced and the $2–4B market access estimate is plausible. This section would survive independent peer review.

## P1 Blocking Items

**P1.1 — Net vs. gross economic calculation.** The $8.2B headline is a gross logistics cost transfer from air to truck. The paper must add: (a) the relay network operating cost (station fees passed to carriers and shippers) subtracted from shipper savings; (b) a clear statement that the $8.2B represents the gross savings before relay network operating costs, with an estimate of net savings to shippers. The net figure is almost certainly still in the $6–8B range (relay operating costs are small relative to air-truck cost differential), but it must be stated. Neumark and Schmitt both flag this as a blocking concern.

**P1.2 — Labor displacement analysis.** The 20,000 relay driver jobs are not net new jobs in trucking; they are a restructuring of the long-haul driver job into six regional relay positions per coast-to-coast trip. The paper must explicitly state: (a) the relay model requires the same number of truck trips and thus the same total driver-hours of trucking labor; (b) what changes is job structure (from long-haul to regional), not job count; (c) the benefit is workforce expansion (a larger pool of workers can participate) and worker welfare improvement (home every night), not net job creation. Neumark flags this as a blocking concern. Failure to address it exposes the paper to a serious methodological criticism that would prevent acceptance at JEP.

## P2 Important Items

**P2.1 — Pharmaceutical cold chain specificity.** The $3.2B addressable pharmaceutical air market needs qualification by drug class. The -70°C exclusion is noted; the paper should also note that some biologics with 2–8°C requirements have regulatory (FDA DSCSA) or carrier-liability considerations that may keep them on air freight regardless of temperature capability. McKinnon flags this.

**P2.2 — Decarbonization co-benefit.** Air freight generates ~50× the CO2 per ton-mile of road freight. The paper should estimate the carbon reduction from $8.2B/year in air-to-truck mode shift and note its relationship to the carbon case made in C.4 for the same relay infrastructure. McKinnon flags this as a material omission.

**P2.3 — NFRZ statutory authority.** Section 7.1 claims FHWA can designate National Freight Relay Zones under NHFP. The specific statutory language supporting this interpretation should be cited, or the paper should acknowledge that new legislation may be required. Puentes flags this.

**P2.4 — $40M capex decomposition.** The $5M per station figure needs a line-item explanation (construction, equipment, technology) and should acknowledge that land acquisition and permitting are not included. Puentes and Walker both raise this.

**P2.5 — Distributional analysis of $8.2B.** A table or paragraph showing the distribution of freight savings across large corporate shippers, small agricultural producers, and consumers (via price pass-through) would address Schmitt's equity concern and strengthen the paper's policy credibility.

**P2.6 — Transit scope separation.** References to relay hub transit node functions should be removed or moved to a forward-reference to F.1/F.2. The relay station is a freight facility; its transit function is a separate design layer analyzed elsewhere. Walker flags this.
