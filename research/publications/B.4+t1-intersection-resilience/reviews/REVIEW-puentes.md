## Overall

The paper produces a tractable investment portfolio with a credible cost and NPV structure, and the diamond interchange zone concept maps reasonably well onto IIJA's National Highway Freight Program (NHFP) and INFRA grant mechanisms. My concern is implementation realism: the paper does not address the multistate coordination challenge for intersections that span state lines, the EIS timeline (typically 6–10 years for major interchange reconstruction), or the tension between the 50-mile access-controlled zone and the discretion of individual state DOTs to set access management standards on their portions of the National Highway System. These are not fatal flaws, but they make the stated $4.5B cost estimate and 2.76:1 B/C ratio more optimistic than the implementation record warrants. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The portfolio structure is well-suited to IIJA funding architecture. NHFP apportioned funds ($8.0B over five years) and INFRA discretionary grants are both eligible for interchange improvements that demonstrably improve freight network performance. The B/C ratio of 2.76:1 clears the informal INFRA competitiveness threshold (projects with B/C ≥ 2.0 have historically shown strong selection rates). The prioritization of Atlanta, Jacksonville, and Toledo is defensible on NHFP criteria: all three are identified as Critical Urban Freight Corridors, and the k=1 vulnerability finding provides a network-level justification that strengthens the grant narrative.

The paper's recommendation to fund the portfolio as a national program rather than 15 individual grants is sound policy advice. FHWA has shown increased interest in corridor-level and network-level grant packaging under IIJA, and a national intersection resilience program would be a logical successor to the National Gateway Clearance Initiative.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper does not address the EIS requirement. A diamond interchange zone — 50 miles of access-controlled managed lanes centered on a major urban interchange — is a major federal action requiring a full EIS under NEPA. For Atlanta I-75/I-85, a prior EIS on the I-285/I-75/I-85 interchange reconstruction took 11 years from NTP to Record of Decision. The paper's phasing plan does not account for this timeline, making the stated construction cost estimates (which assume current labor and material prices) unreliable for projects that will not break ground before 2033 at the earliest.

For intersections that cross state lines — Toledo I-75/I-90 straddles an Ohio DOT and Michigan DOT boundary — the paper does not address interstate compact or programmatic agreement requirements. FHWA can fund multistate projects, but it cannot compel state DOTs to apply uniform access management standards on their respective portions. The 50-mile zone's access control regime requires either a single-state location or a formal multistate agreement.

The IIJA authorization expires in FY2026. The paper should note that funding for a project of this scale would require reauthorization-cycle appropriations, and that the NHFP formula and INFRA program structure may change materially in the next surface transportation bill.

## The Question I'd Push On

For the three priority sites, has the paper identified the current EIS status, any prior NEPA documentation, and the state DOT's current capital program stance toward the proposed improvement? An intersection that is k=1 and analytically justified but where the state DOT has already committed its STIP capacity through 2030 to other projects is not a near-term investment opportunity, regardless of its B/C ratio. The implementation feasibility analysis should be project-specific, not generic.
