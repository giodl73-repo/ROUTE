## Overall

The paper makes an important case for rural infrastructure equity with solid data. The C3 economic opportunity correlation is the most compelling finding. But there are equity dimensions this paper misses: the communities most harmed by interstate construction in the 1950s–70s are not the ones in the gap zones — they are urban communities that were divided, destroyed, or displaced to build the system. The paper's equity framing is one-dimensional: it treats access deficit as the only equity issue, while the history of the interstate system includes severe equity harms from the construction process itself. A stronger paper would acknowledge this.

## What Works

**The 30-mile standard correctly centers rural access.** The paper's choice to focus on geographic access — rather than just traffic volume or freight efficiency — reflects an equity-aware framing that is often absent from infrastructure investment analysis. The three-part rationale (healthcare, agriculture, labor market) grounds the standard in outcomes that matter to rural communities.

**Identifying Type 4 (Economic Opportunity Gaps) as highest priority** is the right equity call. The Appalachian coalfield counties, the Mississippi Delta parishes, and the rural Gulf South counties that appear in both the geographic gap list and the economic disadvantage list are precisely the communities that have been left behind by both the market and by decades of infrastructure underinvestment. Naming them explicitly and ranking I-3 and I-14 first based on who they serve — not just how many people they serve — reflects an equity commitment.

**The paper resists the traffic-volume trap.** I-14's ranking above I-73, and both above higher-volume urban corridors, reflects a deliberate choice to weight access equity over congestion relief. This is the right choice for a gap analysis paper and should be stated more explicitly as a methodological commitment.

## What Doesn't Work

**The equity framing is incomplete.** The paper treats interstate access as uniformly beneficial. But the interstate system's history includes systematic community harm: the routing of I-95 through Overtown in Miami, I-40 through the Brookside neighborhood in Nashville, I-94 through the Rondo neighborhood in St. Paul. These were predominantly Black communities. If the paper is making an equity argument for new corridors, it should acknowledge that previous interstate construction caused equity harm — and commit explicitly to an alignment process for new corridors (particularly I-3 and I-14) that would not repeat those patterns. The interstate history is not just a story of who was left out; it's also a story of who was run over.

**The transit-dependent population is absent from the gap analysis.** The paper measures gap population as all residents of gap counties. But within those counties, the people for whom interstate access matters most are those without cars — the elderly, low-income, and disabled. No-vehicle households (ACS table B08201) should appear in the gap county analysis. A gap county with 10,000 residents and 30% no-vehicle households presents a different equity profile than one with 10,000 residents and 5% no-vehicle households.

**The rural access spur recommendation for Type 3 counties undervalues rural communities.** The paper recommends ≤10 mile spurs for "sparse" Type 3 counties and essentially deprioritizes them. But sparsity is a consequence of historical underinvestment, not a natural condition. Nevada's Elko County (pop. 52,000) is "sparse" relative to its 17,000 sq mi area, but it is an economically active mining and agricultural community with legitimate interstate access needs. Dismissing Type 3 counties because they have low population density accepts the status quo as a given.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper focuses on who lacks access to the existing interstate network. But the equity question goes both ways: which communities had interstate corridors built through them without their consent, and what does the I2.0 investment framework do for them? A complete equity analysis would note that the 9 T1/T1 diamond intersections in the I2.0 framework are predominantly in major metropolitan areas — Atlanta, Boston, San Antonio, Toledo — where communities adjacent to these highways have lived with noise, pollution, and displacement for 50+ years. If I2.0 manages freight lanes to reduce truck traffic on GP lanes, what is the local air quality benefit for communities along I-75 in Detroit or I-10 in Houston? The gap analysis paper frames I2.0 as reaching people who were left out; the equity case is stronger if it also acknowledges those who were run over and shows what I2.0 does for them.
