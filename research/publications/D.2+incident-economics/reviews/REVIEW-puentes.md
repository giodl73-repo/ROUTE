## What Works

The core policy finding — that federal highway funding does not currently have a program category that scores redundancy value as an investment criterion — is correct and important. PROTECT covers climate resilience; NHPP covers state-of-good-repair; the National Highway Freight Program (NHFP) covers freight improvement. None of these programs explicitly scores closure risk reduction or redundancy value as a grant criterion. The paper identifies a genuine funding gap.

The redundancy value framework ($0.5B residual with I-70W alternate, implying $1.9B/yr avoided cost) could be operationalized as a scoring criterion for competitive grants: applicants proposing redundancy routes would calculate the expected annual closure cost of the at-risk corridor and the expected cost reduction from the proposed redundancy, and the ratio of that reduction to project cost would be the benefit score. This is a tractable policy mechanism, and the paper's framework provides the methodology for it.

The top-5 ranking by annual closure cost (Donner $2.4B, Gulf Coast I-10 $1.2B, Dallas Interchange $0.8B, I-95 Baltimore $0.6B, I-35 Oklahoma $0.4B) is the most immediately useful policy table in the paper. These five corridors should be the starting set for any FHWA pilot program on closure cost reduction.

## What Doesn't Work

The paper's PROTECT/NHPP gap analysis is too brief. It identifies that current programs do not score redundancy value, but it does not engage with the question of whether a new program is needed or whether existing programs could be modified. NHFP has a discretionary component with flexible project eligibility; PROTECT discretionary grants can fund resilience-improving projects broadly. Whether either could accommodate a "redundancy value" scoring criterion within existing statutory authority is a question the paper should answer.

The Dallas Interchange ($0.8B/yr) is the second-highest urban entry on the list. Unlike Donner, Dallas is not isolated (B1=5.9 reflects multiple alternate routes). Its high cost is driven by volume × frequency, not isolation. The policy response for Dallas is not redundancy investment — it may be interchange redesign, managed lanes, or demand management. The paper should differentiate the policy recommendations by cost driver type: isolation-dominated (Donner, rural corridors) vs. volume-dominated (Dallas, Baltimore). A single "redundancy investment" framing does not fit all five corridors.

The paper states the funding gap between current closure risk and FHWA program capacity without quantifying it. If the top-15 corridors impose $6.2B/yr in freight cost and the redundancy investment required to halve that cost is estimated at $X billion with a 30-year horizon, what is the annual capital requirement? That comparison — annual closure cost vs. annualized capital cost of redundancy investment — is the fundamental policy arithmetic, and it is absent.

## The Question I'd Push On

The PROTECT program has a discretionary competitive grant component. Has any state DOT submitted a PROTECT application for a redundancy route project — a project whose primary purpose is creating an alternate route to reduce closure risk on an isolated corridor, rather than hardening an existing route? If such applications exist, were they funded? The PROTECT scoring criteria (based on 23 CFR 450) include "resilience improvement" but are focused on hardening existing infrastructure, not building new alternates.

If the existing program architecture excludes new-build redundancy routes from PROTECT eligibility, that is a specific and actionable finding — it means a statutory or regulatory change is required, not just a scoring adjustment. If new-build alternates are already eligible, the question is why states are not applying. The paper should determine which situation applies rather than asserting a funding gap that may be more about state behavior than federal program design.
