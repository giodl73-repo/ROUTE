## Overall

I want to be direct: this paper does real harm if published in its current form. Not because the data are wrong — the gap identification is empirically careful — but because the equity framing is inadequate in ways that matter for policy. The paper argues for investing $292B in new and expanded interstate infrastructure without seriously reckoning with what the last $292B of interstate construction did to communities of color in American cities. That history is not a footnote. It is the context within which any new highway investment proposal must be evaluated. A research paper published in TRR in 2026 that treats "the coverage gap IS an economic opportunity gap" as a complete equity analysis — without asking who was displaced to build the existing system and what safeguards prevent that from happening again — is not ready for publication. ## What Works

The identification of Type 4 gap counties (economically disadvantaged AND geographically isolated) as the highest-priority investment targets is the right equity call. The Appalachian coalfield counties and the Mississippi Delta parishes that score highest on C3 AND appear in the geographic gap analysis are precisely the communities that have been left behind by both market forces and decades of federal underinvestment. This is the paper's strongest equity contribution: it names the places and shows, quantitatively, that infrastructure exclusion and economic disadvantage co-locate.

The 30-mile standard correctly centers rural access rather than urban throughput. The three-part rationale (healthcare, agricultural freight, labor market) is grounded in outcomes that matter to gap communities. The choice to rank corridors by gap population served rather than traffic volume is a meaningful methodological commitment to equity-weighting.

## What Doesn't Work

**The construction-era equity harm is structurally absent.** The paper proposes 12 new or expanded interstate corridors — some through urban areas — without acknowledging that prior interstate construction was one of the most consequential acts of government-sanctioned racial harm in American infrastructure history. I-94 through the Rondo neighborhood in St. Paul destroyed 600 homes and businesses in a thriving Black community. I-40 through the Brookside neighborhood in Nashville eliminated 650 properties. I-95 through Overtown in Miami cut the population of a Black commercial district from 40,000 to 8,000. These are not exceptional cases; they are the pattern. A paper proposing new interstate corridors through or near communities that have seen this history cannot simply omit it. This is not a matter of political sensitivity — it is a matter of research integrity. The paper should include at minimum one paragraph in the Background section acknowledging this history, citing the relevant documentation (Mohl 2002, Foxx 2017), and committing to community impact assessment requirements for any new alignment.

**The equity metric is incomplete.** The paper measures gap population as total county residents. But interstate access matters most for those without cars — the elderly, low-income, and disabled. No-vehicle households (ACS Table B08201) are invisible in this analysis. A gap county with 10,000 residents and 35% no-vehicle households presents a fundamentally different equity profile than one with 10,000 residents and 5% no-vehicle households. The paper cannot claim an equity framing while treating all gap-county residents as equivalent in their highway access needs.

**Proposed alignments are not analyzed for displacement risk.** The paper's proposed corridor alignments (I-3 through Appalachia, I-29S through the Great Plains, I-92 through the Northern Tier) are described in terms of their network endpoints and coverage efficiency. No proposed alignment discusses where the right-of-way would be acquired, whether communities along the alignment have been consulted, or what environmental justice populations are adjacent to the proposed route. For a paper making equity arguments, this omission is disqualifying. At minimum, the paper should note that alignment analysis for each proposed corridor must include an environmental justice screening under Executive Order 12898.

**The Rural West dismissal is inequitable.** Section 5.4 essentially deprioritizes the Rural West as a centroid-artifact problem and recommends "rural access spurs of ≤10 miles." But the indigenous communities in rural Nevada, Montana, and Idaho — the Wind River Reservation (Fremont County, WY), the Navajo Nation in rural New Mexico, the Crow Reservation (Big Horn County, MT) — are not centroid artifacts. They are communities with real and documented transportation access deficits where the gap between the centroid analysis and lived experience runs the other way: the centroid may appear inside the 30-mile threshold while reservation communities are 60+ miles from any interstate. The paper's treatment of the Rural West collapses these distinctions.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper's coverage efficiency metric measures gap population served per billion dollars of investment. But it measures only what the corridors add — not what they might displace. Has the author considered adding a displacement risk score to the corridor ranking? A corridor that serves 40,000 gap-county residents per $B but requires routing through a historically disadvantaged urban neighborhood is not equivalent to one that serves 40,000 rural residents through undeveloped right-of-way. The equity case for I2.0 depends on building corridors that do not repeat the Rondo pattern. What would a displacement-risk-adjusted efficiency metric look like, and would it change the rankings?
