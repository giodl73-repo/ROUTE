## Overall

A well-structured gap analysis with a clear headline number (66.5M, 20.4%) and a defensible taxonomy. The coverage analysis methodology is sound, and the data sources are public and reproducible. My primary concern is the county centroid approach: the paper acknowledges the limitation but does not quantify how badly it misrepresents coverage in large-county states. California's 12.2M "gap population" almost certainly overstates the true access deficit by a large margin. A simple sensitivity analysis using the 10 most affected large-county states would substantially strengthen the paper's credibility.

## What Works

**The 30-mile standard derivation** (Section 3.1): The paper ties the threshold to three independent rationales — emergency response, agricultural freight logistics, and rural labor market access. Each citation is appropriate. The three-rationale convergence around the same threshold is the strongest possible defense of the choice.

**The four-zone taxonomy** (Section 5): The Northern Tier, Appalachians, Rural Gulf South, and Rural West classification is both empirically grounded (from clustering) and intuitively recognizable to practitioners. Reviewers in transportation policy will immediately recognize these zones from their own experience. The Type 1/2/3/4 classification within zones adds useful analytical depth.

**Coverage efficiency metric** (Section 6): Gap population per \$B is a useful, legible metric that enables comparison across very different corridor proposals. The I-3 vs. Southwest Connector comparison (46K/\$B vs. 16K/\$B) makes the investment case transparently.

**Economic opportunity correlation** (Section 4.5): The C3 correlation is the paper's strongest surprise finding. Transportation geography has documented this relationship qualitatively for decades; a quantified correlation from national data gives it new force. The 41% C3 score differential deserves to be more prominent — it should appear in the abstract.

## What Doesn't Work

**California's 12.2M gap population is never adequately resolved.** The paper notes the county centroid effect but treats it as a footnote. San Bernardino County has 2.2M residents, nearly all in the western urban portion — but the county centroid is in the Mojave Desert. Los Angeles County appears as a gap county despite having 100+ miles of interstate. The paper should quantify how many of the 34 California gap counties are centroid-artifact cases versus genuine access deficits. If even half of California's 12.2M gap population is a centroid artifact, the headline national number drops from 20.4% to about 18.4% — still large, but the paper should own this uncertainty explicitly.

**The Rural West is dismissed too quickly.** Section 5.4 treats the Rural West primarily as a county-centroid artifact and recommends only rural access spurs. But Nevada's Elko, Lander, and Humboldt counties have genuine access deficits — mining and agricultural communities 100+ miles from any interstate — that spurs of ≤10 miles cannot address. The paper should distinguish centroid-artifact counties (where a correction would resolve the apparent gap) from genuine isolation counties (where the 30-mile deficit persists even with a tract-level analysis).

**Alignment assumptions in Section 7:** The validation adds proposed corridors to the graph by inserting interchange nodes at 25-mile intervals along a straight-line alignment. Real corridor alignments follow terrain, existing roads, and right-of-way constraints. For mountainous corridors like I-3 through Appalachia, the actual alignment would be significantly longer and serve different counties than a straight line suggests. The sensitivity of coverage estimates to alignment assumptions should be tested.

## The Question I'd Push On

The 30-mile standard is the paper's anchor. But it's applied uniformly across very different geographic contexts. In eastern Montana with flat terrain and clear visibility, a 30-mile drive to an interstate takes 30 minutes. In eastern Kentucky with winding mountain roads, 30 miles might take 50 minutes. If the standard should really be time-based (35 minutes to healthcare; 25 minutes for freight), the geographic threshold should vary by terrain. Has the author tested whether a time-based coverage threshold changes the zone classifications or priority corridor rankings materially?
