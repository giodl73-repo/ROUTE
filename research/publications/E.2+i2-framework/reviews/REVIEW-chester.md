## Overall

The compound exposure hardening component ($18B) signals that the paper takes 2050 climate seriously, and the integration with the D.1 track findings gives that component analytical grounding. But the paper's overall treatment of climate resilience is additive rather than foundational — resilience hardening is one of six components rather than a design constraint that conditions all six. A 30-year infrastructure program built from 2025 to 2055 will operate under climate conditions that differ materially from 2025 conditions in the corridors most affected (Gulf Coast, Ohio Valley, Mountain West). The paper should demonstrate that the I2.0 corridor geometry and design standards are calibrated to 2050 climate projections, not 2025 baseline conditions.

## What Works

The compound exposure hardening component is correctly scoped at $18B and correctly identified as the binding resilience investment for the PTI variance reduction target. The D.1 track finding that compound flood-heat events drive the most extreme PTI spikes (std dev contribution disproportionate to frequency) is the right framing — resilience investment should target tail events, not average conditions.

The PTI variance reduction metric (std dev 0.42→0.18) is an excellent resilience outcome measure precisely because it captures reliability under adverse conditions, not just average performance. The paper is right to use this as the synthesis metric rather than average speed improvement.

The three-phase structure provides a natural resilience update mechanism if Phase 1 hardening is treated as a monitoring and learning investment that informs Phase 2 design standards. The paper could make this explicit.

## What Doesn't Work

The $18B hardening figure is not disaggregated by hazard type or corridor. The appropriate hardening investment for a Gulf Coast corridor subject to Category 4+ hurricane flooding is categorically different from a Mountain West corridor subject to wildfire debris flow. Without corridor-level hazard disaggregation, $18B is a placeholder rather than an estimate. The paper should at minimum show a hazard-by-component breakdown: drainage hardening vs. slope stabilization vs. bridge scour protection vs. pavement heat tolerance.

The 2050 climate integration is asserted rather than demonstrated. The paper states that I2.0 corridors are designed for future climate, but does not show the design standard assumptions (return period flooding, temperature threshold for pavement, sea-level rise allowance for coastal segments). FHWA's Climate Risk and Resilience Planning (CRRP) guidelines and the USDOT's Adaptation Planning guidance provide the framework for this — the paper should cite and apply them.

The five missing link corridors (I-69/I-31/I-29S/I-92/I-70W) traverse multiple high-exposure regions: I-69 crosses the Lower Mississippi floodplain; I-92 (if following a northern alignment) would face freeze-thaw and snow loading; I-70W traverses high-elevation Rocky Mountain passes with significant avalanche and rockfall exposure. Each corridor's hardening requirement should be assessed against its specific compound exposure profile, not averaged into a system-level estimate.

The EV charging infrastructure ($8B) has a direct resilience dependency that is not addressed: EV charging stations in high-flood-risk corridors (Gulf Coast, Atlantic Coast) need elevation and hardening against storm surge. The paper should note whether the $8B includes resilience hardening for charging infrastructure or whether this is an additional cost.

## The Question I'd Push On

The D.1 track identified specific corridors with compound exposure scores in the top quartile. Are the Phase 1 hardening investments ($18B, 2025-2035 phase) concentrated in those corridors? If the highest-exposure corridors are deferred to Phase 2 or Phase 3 because their capital costs are higher, the program will be hardening lower-risk infrastructure first while leaving the highest-risk segments exposed for 10-20 additional years. The paper needs to demonstrate that hardening investment sequencing is exposure-driven, not cost-driven.
