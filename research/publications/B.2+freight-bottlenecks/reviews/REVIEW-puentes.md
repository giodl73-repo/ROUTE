## Overall

The paper's empirical analysis is strong, and the I-285 Atlanta upgrade argument is the most useful policy finding in the paper — it is the kind of evidence-based re-tier case that can actually move a project through federal programming. What the paper lacks is an explicit engagement with the IIJA (Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act) funding framework: if the research is targeting TRR and policy audiences simultaneously, it needs to map the investment recommendations to the actual funding mechanisms available under current law, not a hypothetical Interstate 2.0 program.

## What Works

**The T1/T2 cost paradox framing is policy-legible.** Translating the cascade multiplier into "T2 connectors cause 64% of the bottleneck locations but only 38% of the cost" is the kind of finding that survives a congressional briefing. Policy audiences understand location counts and dollar amounts; they do not understand Spearman ρ. The paper bridges both audiences correctly.

**Atlanta I-285 T1 upgrade case is well-structured.** The argument is precisely the kind of evidence-based tier reclassification that transportation policy needs more of: identify a functional mismatch (corridor doing T1 work under T2 designation), quantify the economic consequence ($3.0B/yr), and propose a specific reclassification with investment implications. This is better evidence than the network topology argument alone, and the paper correctly notes that topology and cost data together are more persuasive than either alone.

**Investment sequencing is operationally plausible.** The three-phase sequencing (Donner Pass tunnel → T1 managed lanes by bottleneck density → T2 relief) is logically ordered and internally consistent with the cost analysis. The Donner Pass 2.5-year payback claim is strong if the $1.6B/yr avoided cost estimate holds up under scrutiny.

**I-40 absence finding has direct policy utility.** Identifying I-40 as a corridor where managed lanes are not the priority investment is as useful as identifying I-95 as the top priority — it prevents misallocation. Policymakers often assume that T1 corridors all have the same investment needs; the paper correctly shows they do not.

## What Doesn't Work

**No engagement with IIJA funding categories.** The IIJA (P.L. 117-58, 2021) provides $110B for highways over 5 years, including specific programs for freight (INFRA grants, MPDG, National Highway Freight Program) and for megaproject delivery (Nationally Significant Freight and Highway Projects). If the I-285 T1 upgrade or the I-95 managed lane investment is to move from research to implementation, it needs a plausible federal funding path. The paper recommends these investments without addressing whether they are programmable under current law, what the federal cost-share would be, or whether they require new legislative authority (which Interstate 2.0 as a program would). TRR's policy audience reads for implementation relevance, not just diagnosis.

**Donner Pass $4B estimate and $1.6B/yr avoided cost need citation and methodology.** Section 8.1 cites a "$4B freight tunnel" at Donner Pass with a "2.5-year payback at $1.6B/yr in avoided weather closure costs." These numbers are consequential — they are the basis for the paper's highest-priority investment recommendation — but they are not sourced in this paper. Are they from the ROUTE E.2 framework paper? From a Caltrans feasibility study? From the ROUTE analysis itself? The payback calculation (50 closures/yr × 18 hrs × truck volume × $225/hr rerouting premium from Section 3.4) should be shown explicitly, not summarized.

**The I-285 T1 upgrade argument slips between rubric evidence and policy argument.** The paper states that the bottleneck data provides "the economic justification that pure network topology does not" for the I-285 T1 upgrade. This is correct — but the paper then proceeds to recommend upgrading I-285 as if the rubric question is settled. The rubric has a B2 (Network Centrality) instability problem (documented in A.2) that specifically affects I-285's centrality score. If the B2 score for I-285 is an underestimate because the Atlanta sub-graph is in a state with incomplete TIGER/Line coverage, the topology case may be stronger than currently scored. The paper should acknowledge this and note that the B2 recomputation in v1.3 may independently support the I-285 T1 reclassification.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper recommends I-285 T1 designation based on functional behavior and bottleneck cost data. But T1 designation in the ROUTE framework implies managed freight lanes — a specific infrastructure investment. If I-285 is upgraded to T1 and receives managed freight lanes, what happens to the T1/T2 transfer dynamics at the I-75/I-285 and I-85/I-285 interchanges? Does T1-to-T1 capacity at those nodes resolve the bottleneck, or does it shift the bottleneck to the next interchange downstream? The Atlanta cluster has four bottleneck locations within 15 miles; managed lanes on one segment may simply relocate rather than eliminate the bottleneck. Has the sequential bottleneck relocation risk been examined?
