## Overall

This paper addresses a genuine gap in the transportation economics literature: the systematic documentation of county-level interstate access deficits and their correlation with agricultural and rural economic disadvantage. As someone who has spent three decades studying rural labor markets in the Midwest and Great Plains, I find the geographic gap identification compelling and the four-zone taxonomy empirically credible. The Northern Tier and Appalachian gap zones reflect patterns I recognize from regional labor market research. My concerns are about the RUCC classification accuracy, the treatment of agricultural freight access within the gap analysis, and the quality of the rural-specific corridor justifications.

## What Works

The C3 economic opportunity correlation is the paper's most important finding for a rural economist. The 41% higher C3 score in gap counties — confirmed across multiple zone types — tells us something important: interstate access and economic opportunity co-locate not by chance but through the reinforcing mechanisms of freight access, labor market integration, and supply chain connectivity. The paper correctly identifies this as a gap in economic participation, not merely in road coverage.

The I-3 and I-14 corridor rankings reflect sound rural economic logic. Both corridors serve agricultural production zones (Appalachian corridor including WV/KY tobacco and poultry; Texas/Gulf Coast corridor including Gulf petrochemical and agricultural export zones) where freight access is a binding constraint. The coverage efficiency metric correctly ranks these above higher-population but lower-gap-density corridors.

The agricultural freight rationale for the 30-mile standard (Section 3.1) is well-calibrated. The 30-mile harvest transport radius for grain trucks is the correct planning parameter for corn-soybean country; it is somewhat generous for specialty crops (vegetables, fruit) but conservative for cattle country where livestock trucks routinely cover 60-mile hauls. The three-rationale convergence is appropriate.

## What Doesn't Work

**The RUCC classification is applied without verification.** The paper uses USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) to classify counties in the gap analysis, but it does not verify that the RUCC classification is current for all 1,510 gap counties. RUCC codes are updated only after decennial censuses, and several counties in the analysis zone have undergone classification changes between 2013 and 2023 due to micropolitan area reclassification. Most significantly, some Appalachian coal counties that appear as RUCC 4-6 (small metro adjacent to metro) in the 2013 codes are now more appropriately classified as RUCC 7-9 (completely rural) after population loss in the 2010s. If the paper's gap zone population counts rely on outdated RUCC codes, the rural-versus-urban split in gap counties is miscalibrated. The paper should verify against 2020 RUCC codes (released 2023) and report if any reclassifications affect the corridor rankings.

**Agricultural freight access is not in the corridor efficiency metric.** The coverage efficiency metric (gap population / $B) treats all gap-county residents as equivalent. But a gap county in the Central Plains with 25,000 residents and 2 million bushels/year of corn export production generates very different economic value from interstate access than a gap county in rural Appalachia with 25,000 residents and no agricultural export base. USDA's NASS agricultural census data at the county level could provide a freight value dimension — even a crude one — that would differentiate agricultural-freight-intensive gap counties from general-population gap counties. The paper should at minimum note this omission and its implication for corridor rankings.

**The Northern Tier gap analysis underweights agricultural freight.** The Northern Tier gap zone (ND/MT/MN) is identified primarily as a population access deficit. But the agricultural freight argument for this zone is at least as strong as the population argument: North Dakota is the #1 US producer of spring wheat, durum, sunflowers, and canola; Montana is a top producer of winter wheat and barley. The absence of a continuous east-west interstate across the Northern Tier imposes real freight costs — grain trucks traveling to export terminals in Duluth or to processing facilities in Minneapolis use US-2, a two-lane highway that crosses multiple state lines and lacks interstate load ratings in several segments. The paper mentions this corridor but does not quantify the agricultural freight benefit, which I suspect would be the strongest NPV driver for the Northern Tier corridor, exceeding the population coverage efficiency argument.

**The I-29S/US-83 Great Plains corridor is underanalyzed.** The Great Bend KS to Laredo TX corridor is listed as a proposed T1 corridor but receives only brief treatment relative to the Northern Tier and I-3. This corridor crosses the most productive wheat, sorghum, and cattle country in the US — the High Plains of Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas — with agricultural freight volumes that rival any corridor in the proposed set. The paper should give this corridor treatment comparable to the Northern Tier.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper measures coverage efficiency as gap population served per $B. For rural and agricultural corridors, this metric systematically undervalues freight access: it counts people but not tons. A wheat county with 8,000 residents and 50 million bushels per year of export production generates more freight value from interstate access than a suburban-fringe gap county with 25,000 residents and minimal agricultural output. Have you run the corridor rankings using a freight-ton-weighted efficiency metric, and would it produce a different priority order? My expectation is that the Northern Tier and Great Plains corridors would move substantially up the ranking, while some of the Appalachian Type 2 corridors (which serve population gaps but modest freight flows) would move down.
