## Overall

The selection of NY→LA and HOU→CHI as the study corridors is not adequately motivated. These are intuitive choices — they represent major inter-regional movements — but the paper does not demonstrate that these two corridors account for a disproportionate share of the national freight reliability problem, or that they represent the policy-relevant margin for I2.0 investment. A stronger paper would show why these two corridors (rather than, say, I-10 Gulf Coast or I-5 Pacific Northwest) produce the most actionable findings. Score: 3/4.

## What Works

The paper does good work within its chosen corridors. The binding constraint analysis for NY→LA — Donner Pass as geographic limit, Bay Area urban network as capacity limit — is a useful decomposition that identifies two distinct failure modes requiring different interventions (one is a single-point geographic bottleneck; the other is a distributed urban congestion problem). These are correctly treated as separate problems, not the same problem at different scales.

The HOU-CHI corridor analysis effectively demonstrates the routing inefficiency created by the absence of I-69: the three-hop detour through Dallas and St. Louis imposes a 240-mile penalty on every truck movement between the Gulf and the upper Midwest. This is a strong, geography-grounded finding.

The PTI framework, despite its methodological limitations noted by other reviewers, is an appropriate way to connect link-level performance to shipper decision-making. The SLA window concept (95th-percentile planning buffer) is a useful policy translation.

## What Doesn't Work

The O-D pair selection deserves more explicit justification. Why NY→LA rather than SFO→NYC? These are different flows: NY→LA is dominated by general merchandise and retail distribution; SFO→NYC has a much higher technology and perishables component, which drives different time-value profiles. The paper treats the Manhattan-to-Los Angeles frame as self-evident, but from a freight geography perspective, the westbound-versus-eastbound imbalance on I-80 is significant — empty return movements on the NY→LA corridor are not symmetric with loaded westbound movements, and the PTI calculation should reflect directional flow.

The air freight competition for the NY-LA corridor is not addressed. A non-trivial share of high-value freight in the NY-LA lane — electronics, pharmaceuticals, time-sensitive retail — moves by air through JFK-LAX. If I2.0 managed lanes primarily benefit medium-value freight that is too expensive for air but constrained by truck PTI, the reliability benefit accrues to a specific segment of the freight market that the paper does not characterize. This matters for the $5.7B reliability cost: that figure presumably includes freight that will never use the managed lane regardless of PTI improvement.

The paper also does not address the spatial geography of the binding constraints. Donner Pass is a point bottleneck — a single mountain crossing — and the intervention (managed lane approach) would not physically relieve the geographic constraint, only manage access to the constrained capacity. A Donner Pass bypass would be a different kind of intervention. The paper conflates two different types of bottleneck without distinguishing the appropriate intervention for each.

## The Question I'd Push On

If these two corridors represent the highest-priority reliability investment opportunities, what is the ranking methodology, and how do other major O-D pairs (e.g., Chicago-Dallas, Miami-Atlanta, Seattle-Portland) compare on PTI and annual reliability cost? The paper's implicit claim is that NY-LA and HOU-CHI are the right corridors to analyze first — but this claim is asserted, not demonstrated. A brief comparison against a set of candidate O-D pairs, ranked by annual reliability cost or PTI, would substantially strengthen the corridor selection rationale.
