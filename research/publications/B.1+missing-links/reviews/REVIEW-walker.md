## Overall

The gap analysis methodology is rigorous and the headline finding is important. My concern is structural: the paper's 30-mile standard measures highway access, and then the paper treats highway access as equivalent to mobility access. These are not the same thing, and conflating them produces a flawed equity framing. The 3.2 million Americans identified as "beyond 30 miles of any interstate" include a substantial population of people who cannot or do not drive — for whom a new interstate 25 miles away is no more useful than one 125 miles away. The paper needs to either (a) reframe its claim as "vehicle-based access" throughout, or (b) add a transit-access analysis that shows what the identified gaps mean for transit-dependent travelers. As written, it cannot claim to address mobility equity without this distinction. Score: 2/4.

## What Works

The 30-mile standard derivation is honest about its vehicle-centric basis. The emergency response rationale (35-minute ambulance response time) and agricultural freight rationale are explicitly vehicle-dependent. The labor market access rationale is the only one that could apply to non-drivers, and only if intercity transit service connects gap-county residents to the interstate corridor. The paper is not wrong to use this standard — it is wrong to draw equity conclusions from it without acknowledging its scope.

The four-zone taxonomy is useful regardless of mode. The Northern Tier, Appalachian, Gulf South, and Rural West gap zones are genuine access deserts for all travelers, not just drivers. A transit planner looking at these zones would identify them as equally underserved by any form of intercity mobility. The paper's geographic analysis is sound; the mode-specific framing of the conclusions is the problem.

The C3 economic opportunity correlation (41% higher in gap counties) is robust to the mode issue. Economic disadvantage co-locates with geographic access deficits for reasons that predate the automobile — these are communities that lack rail, bus, and highway access simultaneously. The finding strengthens the case for any mode of intercity access investment, not just interstate highways.

## What Doesn't Work

**The 30-mile highway access standard is not a mobility access standard.** The paper's title is "Missing Links: Gap Analysis of the US Interstate Network." The paper's conclusions include statements like "3.2 million Americans currently lack adequate transportation access." These two framings are not equivalent. Highway access is a necessary condition for automobile-based mobility, not a sufficient condition for all-mode mobility. The 66.5 million figure (and the corrected 48.8 million figure) describes a highway access deficit, not a transportation access deficit. The paper should be explicit about this distinction throughout, and should remove or qualify any language that conflates highway access with transportation access.

**Transit-dependent population is invisible.** The paper identifies 3,139 county centroids and measures distance to the nearest interstate interchange. It does not ask what fraction of gap-county residents are transit-dependent — elderly, disabled, low-income, or simply car-free by choice. In some gap zones this fraction is high: rural Appalachian counties have elevated elderly populations; rural Gulf South counties have elevated poverty and vehicle-ownership rates. A new interstate 25 miles from Breathitt County, Kentucky does not improve the mobility of an elderly resident of Jackson who cannot drive. The paper should add ACS no-vehicle household data (Table B08201) to the gap county analysis and acknowledge the fraction of "gap population" for whom the gap metric is irrelevant.

**The I2.0 transit facility claim is not integrated into the gap analysis.** The paper references I2.0 "shared transit facilities" (Section 1) as a feature of the proposed corridor standard. If I2.0 corridors include intercity bus stop facilities and park-and-ride access, then the mobility access question is partially addressed — but the paper never closes this loop. Either show how I2.0's transit integration features would serve gap-county transit-dependent residents (how far is the nearest park-and-ride from a gap county center? what intercity bus service would connect to it?), or remove the transit facility reference as a feature not analyzed in this paper.

**The rural labor market access rationale conflates driving with commuting.** Section 3.1 argues that the 30-mile standard is justified by labor market access — workers can reach employment centers within a reasonable commute. But rural labor market access for non-drivers requires transit connections, not just highway proximity. The counties in the gap zone with the highest transit-dependency rates (rural Gulf South, rural Appalachia) are precisely those where the labor market access argument should center intercity bus service, not highway proximity.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper ranks proposed corridors by coverage efficiency: gap population served per billion dollars of investment. If I2.0 corridors include intercity bus stops at 50-mile intervals (as the I2.0 spec suggests), then each new corridor also creates a transit-accessible node network for gap-county residents. Has the author estimated the transit catchment population — the fraction of gap-county residents within 10 miles of a proposed I2.0 corridor transit stop? That number would show how much of the gap can be served for transit-dependent travelers as a byproduct of the highway investment, and it would transform the equity case for I2.0 from "highway access" to "multimodal access."
