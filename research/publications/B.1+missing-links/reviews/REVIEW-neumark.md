## Overall

This paper makes a genuine and reproducible empirical contribution: the first systematic population-weighted county-level coverage gap analysis of the US interstate system, with a defensible headline number and a ranked priority corridor list. The gap identification methodology is sound. My concern is with the economic interpretation: the paper's most prominent economic claim — that "the coverage gap IS an economic opportunity gap" — is not established by the analysis. The C3 correlation is descriptive. Establishing that interstate access causes economic disadvantage rather than co-locating with it requires identification strategies the paper does not employ. This is a solvable problem, and the paper's credibility would increase significantly if the authors made a more careful distinction between what the data show and what the data imply. Score: 3/4 — good paper with a specific fixable problem.

## What Works

The headline number is reproducible and credible. 66.5M (20.4%) from publicly available Census and TIGER data, with a corrected lower bound of 48.8M (14.8%) after artifact removal, gives the paper two defensible estimates bracketing the true value. The transparency about this range is methodologically honest.

The corridor ranking methodology (coverage efficiency: gap population / $B) is appropriate for a gap analysis paper. It is explicitly not an NPV analysis, which is the right choice given the difficulty of estimating economic development multipliers for proposed corridors. The paper correctly limits its claim to "which corridors serve the most unserved population per dollar" without over-claiming about economic returns.

The four-zone taxonomy is useful and durable. These zones will be recognizable to transportation economists, regional scientists, and policy practitioners. The Northern Tier / Appalachians / Gulf South / Rural West classification could serve as a standard for future corridor-level research in this space.

## What Doesn't Work

**The causal claim for the C3 correlation is not established.** The paper states in multiple places that the coverage gap "IS an economic opportunity gap" and that gap counties are economically disadvantaged "because of" their access deficits. The supporting evidence is a 41% C3 score differential between gap and non-gap counties. This is descriptive correlation. The identification problem is severe: eastern Kentucky coal counties are economically disadvantaged for reasons that predate the interstate era (coal industry decline, educational attainment patterns, out-migration) and would persist even with full interstate access. Nevada and Montana gap counties are economically constrained by geography and industry structure, not by road access. The paper should cite Michaels (2008) — the most credible causal identification of interstate access effects, using the 1947 Federal Aid Highway Act plan as an instrument — and present the C3 correlation as descriptive evidence consistent with the hypothesis, not as proof of it. The paper should also cite Chandra and Thompson (2000) for the county-level employment effects. Making this distinction does not weaken the paper — it strengthens it by showing the authors understand the identification problem.

**The cost estimates need wider uncertainty bands.** The $30M/mile upgrade and $75M/mile greenfield figures are reasonable medians for flat-terrain corridors. The stated sensitivity range (+50%/-30%) is far too narrow for mountainous corridors. I-40 through the Smoky Mountains cost approximately $120M/mile in 2010 dollars. I-70 through Vail Pass cost $185M/mile in 2011 dollars for reconstruction. A Northern Tier corridor across the Montana Rockies or an I-3 corridor through the central Appalachians would be in this range, not the $75M/mile base case. The paper should present a "difficult terrain" cost scenario that uses $150-200M/mile for mountain segments and show how this affects the corridor efficiency rankings. My expectation is that I-3's ranking falls significantly once realistic Appalachian terrain costs are applied.

**The efficiency metric doesn't account for induced economic activity.** The paper ranks corridors by static gap population served — how many people currently in gap counties would move inside the 30-mile threshold. It does not account for the economic activity that interstate access induces: new firms locating in newly-accessible counties, workers commuting to employment centers they previously couldn't reach, agricultural producers gaining access to freight networks. The literature on induced demand from highway access (Baum-Snow 2007 on suburbanization; Chandra and Thompson 2000 on rural employment) suggests that the true economic return to a new corridor is substantially larger than the static coverage metric captures. The paper should note this as a limitation and acknowledge that the coverage efficiency ranking is a lower bound on total social return.

## The Question I'd Push On

The paper's efficiency ranking maximizes gap population served per dollar. Suppose instead you maximized expected economic development benefit per dollar — using the Michaels (2008) employment multiplier for counties gaining interstate access. Would the corridor priority order change? My prior, based on what we know about which county types respond most to new highway access, is that the agricultural and rural manufacturing counties in the Appalachian and Gulf South gap zones would show higher economic development returns than the low-density Montana/Nevada gap counties — which means the coverage-efficiency ranking and the economic-development ranking might converge. But it might not. The paper would be substantially stronger if it showed this calculation, or explained why it cannot be done with available data.
